## Definition

Dogfooding — using your own unfinished product internally to build and improve it — is described by [[Andrew Ambrosino]] as central to how [[OpenAI]]'s [[Codex]] app was and continues to be developed.

## Key Information

- Ambrosino's original personal goal when building the first Codex app was to make it good enough that he could write the Codex app's own code with it ("a super quick dogfooding loop"): fix what's broken so you can do the next thing, repeatedly.
- Team-wide practice: "there is a desire among all of us to try to do as much as possible in the app even when it's not the best tool so that it can become the best tool" — including using Codex to manage the coordination/release process for Codex's own features (e.g., an internal release tracker automated by gathering updates from pull requests and Slack channels).
- Ambrosino explicitly notes the tension this creates: "we often don't improve our process so that we can make the product better to do it," calling it "a deeply uncomfortable place to be in."
- Cited as the mechanism by which the Codex org discovered internal product-market fit before public release: in January/February, engineering and research teams inside OpenAI began using an early Codex app heavily and loved it, which gave the team confidence to raise the quality bar and ship externally.
- Distinguished from a simple beta test: dogfooding here is described as continuous and structural — the org's own workflow (planning, tracking, release management) is itself run through the product being built.

### Fiona Fung's application across her career (Anthropic, Meta, Microsoft)

- [[Fiona Fung]] traces her love of dogfooding back to Microsoft's Visual Studio editor team, where she used the VS editor to build the VS editor itself, aiming to create a delightful experience for herself and teammates who were also heavy VS users — giving the team unusually fast internal feedback loops even before social media enabled fast external customer feedback.
- At [[Meta]], she continued the practice across very different products (VR/AR, [[Facebook Marketplace]], Instagram): e.g., she personally discovered a new resale-scam vector by trying to sell her own MacBook Air on Marketplace, and repeatedly found reproducible floor-height/tracking issues while testing VR/AR setups herself.
- At [[Anthropic]], she uses her own [[Claude Code]] and [[Claude Cowork]] sessions daily (not just to generate PRs, but to review team output and hold coaching conversations), and treats her own use of Cowork for personal tasks (e.g., travel expensing) as a discovery mechanism for new product opportunities — directly leading to the "Claude for Small Business" bundle (see [[Latent Demand]]).
- General principle she draws from this: anecdotal, first-hand product experience ("trust the anecdote") surfaces blockers and opportunities that dashboards and metrics alone miss — illustrated by a Facebook Marketplace regional launch in Chile that was blocked by poor LTE performance, discovered only via an in-person research trip.
- Advice for leaders who cannot directly use the product they oversee: substitute direct customer visits, which she says reliably surface new learning every time.
