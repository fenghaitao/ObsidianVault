## Definition

"Don't make me think" is a product design mantra (from Steve Krug's book of the same name) that Stewart Butterfield elevated as a core principle at Slack. It holds that the goal of design is to prevent users from having to think in order to use software, because thinking is cognitively expensive and emotionally risky.

## Key Information

- **Two reasons this matters**:
  1. **Cognitive cost**: making decisions literally burns glucose — there's a metabolic cost to thinking (ATP created in mitochondria, decision fatigue)
  2. **Emotional cost**: if software stops you and asks you to make a decision you don't understand, it makes you feel stupid. Users blame themselves, not the software
- **The "parents" example**: when people under 50 talk to their parents about using software, the parents almost always feel stupid — they're the ones who are wrong
- **Connection to comprehension vs. friction**: "don't make me think" is Butterfield's replacement for "reduce friction" as the primary design goal
- **Connection to clicks/taps**: reducing the number of clicks is almost always the wrong goal. You could make every action a single click by exposing everything on one screen — obviously terrible. The real goal is making every single interaction trivially easy
- **Snapchat example**: a teenager tapping 4-7 times per second for 6 minutes — every interaction was trivially easy, not requiring thought. If Snapchat's goal had been to reduce taps, it would have ruined the experience
- **Uber example**: "Where would you like to go?" + "Other" — trivially easy because almost all the time, people just want one thing
- **Practical technique**: "Stop what you're doing, close your eyes, take a couple deep breaths, pretend you're a natural human being, open your eyes, and look at this thing. Can you figure out what it's supposed to do?"
