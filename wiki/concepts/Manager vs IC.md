## Definition

Manager vs IC is the career decision framework for choosing between the management track and the individual contributor track in software engineering. Ricky's experience as a Tech Lead Manager at Google provides insight into the trade-offs of each path.

## Key Information

### The IC Track Advantages
- As a senior IC, Ricky had "great work-life balance" — flexible schedule, midday naps, gym at off-peak hours
- As a TL (not a manager), you get "all the fun parts" — helping people grow, guiding projects, mentoring — without the administrative burden
- IC work involves less overhead and fewer meetings

### The Manager Track Overhead
- Difficult feedback conversations when people are not performing well
- Writing performance reviews and attending calibration sessions
- More meetings — "6 hours or 8 hours of back-to-back meetings or one-on-ones"
- Schedule becomes more rigid and less flexible
- Social awkwardness of managing people, especially when younger

### The Rewards of Management
- More rewarding when you see your reports get promoted — "because you are their manager, you help them get promoted"
- A more formal role means clear boundaries on who you're responsible for
- Ricky's concrete example: he was informally helping 20 new hires; becoming a manager limited his formal responsibility to 4, actually giving him more time back

### The Decision Framework
- The fun parts of helping people grow are available as a TL without being a manager
- The higher you go (L6, L7, L8), the more meetings you'll have regardless of IC or manager track — "the influence you have is always going to be through other people"
- Ricky's advice: consider whether you actually want the responsibilities of management, or if you just enjoy mentoring
- He wished he had waited longer before becoming a manager

### Rahul's Manager vs IC Decision
- At Meta, Rahul had explicit conversations with his manager about IC vs management growth
- The conclusion was to first go up the IC ladder (to IC6) and then try management, which is what happened
- He found the TLM role unsustainable long-term because you're unclear about whether you're evaluated on IC work or people leadership
- He described the management shift as becoming "more of a firefighter" — more reactive rather than proactive, less able to guard your time
- Step one for deciding: ask yourself if you genuinely enjoy helping people and if you dread one-on-ones — if so, management will be difficult
- Step two: do you have opinions about how the team and organization should change? Best managers come in with a strong point of view
- Test management by doing one-on-ones and leading meetings as an IC before committing
- A senior IC's expectation is already to have clear opinions about what the team should do for the next 6-12 months — if you don't want those negotiations, management isn't a good fit
- The TLM role is best as a transitory role, not a long-term position

### Philip Su's Manager vs IC Experience
- Philip Su switched between IC and management tracks at least six times in his career
- His order of preference: (1) be an IC, (2) be a manager if the team is poorly managed and doesn't have a better manager
- He initially pursued management because he came from a culture (ethnically Chinese) where managers were more respected and would get further in career — he later called this "somewhat misguided"
- He kept the two-way door open by continuing to code while managing, which he says most people cannot do
- He deliberately sought a demotion from E9 (Site Director) to E7 (IC at Oculus) when switching back, accepting the level drop and salary reduction
- Four separate times, a previous manager asked to report to Philip — he became their manager
- His biggest management weakness: giving feedback. He pulls punches, resists giving feedback due to fear of confrontation, and downplays how important feedback is
- His management strength: motivating action. When he believes in something, he's passionate and communicates that passion and vision in a way that excites people
- At the senior levels (E8+), Philip observes a "diamond shape" convergence where IC and manager skills overlap — both require vision, communication, and strategic thinking
- His advice: before becoming a manager, ask "would I want to work for me?" and be sure you're not walking through a one-way door
