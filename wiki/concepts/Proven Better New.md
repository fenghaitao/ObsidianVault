## Definition

Product-development framework developed by [[Mark Pincus]] at [[Zynga]] (originally the working title of his book [[Life at the Speed of Play]]). Holds that human instincts are right ~95% of the time, but the ideas built on top of those instincts are wrong ~75% of the time — so successful products should be built in three deliberate layers: **Proven** (master an existing pattern that already works, precisely, on the specific platform/audience), **Better** (make a version so improved that 10 out of 10 existing users of the proven product would say "hell yeah, I'd switch"), and **New** (add one small, genuinely novel element — accepting that this element is the most likely part to fail).

## Key Information

- **Proven** must be precise: "proven" only counts if it was proven on the exact platform, for the exact audience, at the exact experience level being targeted — not just "a game that was popular in the 90s." Founders who misuse "proven" to justify a weak idea are misapplying the framework.
- **Better** is usually small, incremental, and disproportionately noticed by power/intense users — not a big ambitious leap (which the framework calls "new," and warns founders are usually over-ambitious/wrong about).
- **New** is the part most likely to fail, and the framework explicitly expects that: build the product assuming the new idea will probably not work, and have several backup "new" ideas ready to test.
- Case studies: [[Words With Friends]] (proven=Scrabble, better=mobile polish, new=Facebook-graph asynchronous play with friends); the [[iPhone]] (proven=phone/iPod patterns, better=refinement, new=touchscreen, per the MIT/[[Steve Jobs]] TED anecdote); [[Slack]] (possibly proven+better with minimal new); [[OMG Pop]]'s [[Draw Something]] (proven=copied Words With Friends turn-based mechanic) vs. [[Rovio]]'s [[Angry Birds]] (pure "new," no proven/better — high-variance "wildcat drilling").
- [[Sid Meier]]'s Facebook game is the cautionary counter-example: excellent "new" game design failed commercially because the product skipped "proven" onboarding patterns (first-time-user-experience).
- Related to "[[Moral Arbitrage]]": founders resist the "proven" step because copying feels like cheating, which the framework argues is a mistaken, ego-driven resistance — ambition should be defined by what serves the end consumer, not peer/industry respect.
- Feeds into "[[Kill Hope Before Hope Kills You]]": because the "new" layer is expected to fail most of the time, teams should treat early product bets as hypotheses to test cheaply and fast (especially using AI), not as beliefs to be defended.
