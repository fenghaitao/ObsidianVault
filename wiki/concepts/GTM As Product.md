## Definition

[[Jeanne DeWitt Grosser]]'s (COO, [[Vercel]]) framework for treating go-to-market itself as a designed product: map the full customer journey from first awareness through multi-year retention the way a product manager maps a user journey, and deliberately design distinctive, high-value experiences at each step rather than transactional, forgettable sales interactions.

## Key Information

- **Origin**: Grosser traces the idea to her early career on Google's original Gmail team (2004) — watching a massive technical advantage (a full year's lead over Yahoo Mail, longer over Hotmail) erode as competitors caught up, leading her to conclude that as products commoditize, "the experience of being sold to" becomes the actual differentiator, since people buy things in part because of how they feel about them.
- **Concrete example**: at Stripe, the first sales call was reframed from a standard discovery Q&A into a hands-on architecture-whiteboarding session, where the prospect drew out their actual payments/technical stack — the customer left with a useful artifact (their own architecture diagram) and a sense of genuine collaboration, while Stripe learned deeply about the prospect's stack and where value/competition lay.
- **Adding value at every touchpoint regardless of outcome**: even prospects who don't buy should leave with something useful (e.g., Vercel proactively shares site-performance/SEO benchmarking data during outreach); customers frequently return years later during a subsequent buying cycle, so no interaction is wasted.
- **Multi-dimensional segmentation** (the analytical backbone of GTM-as-product): beyond simple size buckets (SMB/mid-market/enterprise), Grosser layers in growth potential (crucial for consumption-based pricing — a 200%-year-over-year grower is more valuable to target than an 8% grower), "promote" signals like web-traffic rank (a smaller company with outsized traffic gets treated as enterprise, since it correlates with revenue potential), and workload/business-model type (B2B vs. B2C vs. platform/marketplace at Stripe; e-commerce vs. SaaS vs. crypto at Vercel) — each combination implying a different buying process, product need, and sales content.
- Recommends starting formal segmentation work in a company's first 30 days in a new GTM role, and treating segmentation as a company-wide discipline (not just a GTM one) — e.g., every new Vercel hire receives a "know your customer" (KYC) briefing on the segmentation framework in their first week, so product managers instinctively know which segment they're building for.
- **Rule of thumb**: roughly three segmentation attributes is a workable starting point — enough to be meaningfully specific (e.g., "Series A, VC-backed, marketplace business model") without fragmenting a small sales team across too many micro-segments.
- **Buying psychology**: cites a stat that ~80% of customers buy to avoid pain or reduce risk, not to chase upside — meaning positioning built around "here's the exciting future" mostly resonates with fellow founders, while positioning built around risk avoidance (missing a revenue target, losing to competitors, career risk of a bad vendor choice) converts far more broadly, especially in enterprise.
