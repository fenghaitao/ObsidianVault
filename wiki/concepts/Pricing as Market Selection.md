## Definition

Pricing as Market Selection is Jason Cohen's framework arguing that pricing doesn't just affect demand along a curve — it selects which market segment you serve. Raising prices often doesn't change signup rates because you enter a different market where the higher price signals quality and appropriateness.

## Why the Microeconomic Demand Curve Is Wrong

The textbook demand curve says: raise price → demand goes down. But in practice:

- **Low prices repel certain buyers.** A $2/month or $100/month product looks inadequate to a mid-market company with 1,000 employees and $400M revenue. They assume it can't be good enough, support won't be adequate, governance won't be in place.
- **Raising prices can increase signups.** This happens "all the time" — even for solo entrepreneurs on Twitter. By entering a higher price range, you enter a market that takes the product seriously.
- **The actual curve is a mesa, not a line.** It slopes up, stays flat, then slopes down. Only the very lowest end of the market follows the traditional demand curve slope.

## The Double Down Example (Positioning 8x Multiplier)

Same product, two different positioning approaches:

| Positioning | Pitch | Willing to Pay | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cost savings | "Cut AdWords costs in half" | $5K/month (on $40K spend) | Saving $20K, willing to give 25% of savings |
| Growth | "Double your leads" | $40K/month | Already willing to spend $40K for this many leads — doubling them is worth $40K |

**Key insight:** Sell what the CEO values (growth) rather than what saves money. The cap on "saving money" is an order of magnitude lower than the cap on "delivering more of what they already value."

## Jason Cohen's Anecdote: 12x Price Increase

A founder selling enterprise software for $300/year was told to change it to $300/month (12x increase). Signups per week stayed the same (1-2). Jason's response: "What you're going to do is raise prices again. You just 12xed the price and nothing observable changed. That means you're not near the price yet."

## Caveats

- Pricing is not a knob you can turn independently. Different markets have different demands (SOC 2, governance, integrations, professional services).
- The market you're in may be the only one where your differentiation is valuable.
- It can be cultural: Buffer chose not to go upmarket because they are "a company for the little people."
- Pricing structure and positioning are as important as the number itself.
