## Definition

Segmentation is the process of carving up the universe of companies to reason about where they buy differently. It should be a company-wide framework (not just a GTM concern) and should go beyond simple size-based categories to include multiple axes that reflect how value is delivered and how buying behavior differs.

## Key Information

- Traditional segmentation: SMB (single decision maker), Mid-Market (small team), Enterprise (complex, committee-driven). This is rational but insufficient alone.
- Jeanne DeWitt Grosser recommends a multi-axis graph approach:
  - **Stripe's axes:** Company size (x-axis) × Growth potential (y-axis, important for consumption-based models) × Business model (B2B, B2C, B2B2B/platform, B2B2C/marketplace).
  - **Vercel's axes:** Company size × Growth potential × CrUX rank (Google traffic data, observable via Chrome) × Workload type (e-commerce, crypto, SaaS, etc.).
- Observable attributes matter: CrUX rank is publicly available and correlates with Vercel revenue, making it a powerful segmentation dimension.
- Example: OpenAI (~3,000 employees = mid-market by size, but a top-25 traffic site = enterprise by Vercel's segmentation).
- How to develop: sit with data science, identify what drives revenue (what correlates with $100K vs $1M customers), and identify clusters where you're winning repeatedly.
- Segmentation is a "company thing" — every new hire at Vercel learns the segmentation framework (tied to the KYC/Know Your Customer value) so that product managers, engineers, and GTM all speak the same language.
- A heuristic: aim for ~3 attributes to narrow ICP — more than that becomes unwieldy for small teams.
- Good segmentation reveals gaps: Vercel discovered strong e-commerce penetration but low enterprise SaaS penetration (because those companies were built before Vercel existed), informing where to focus.
