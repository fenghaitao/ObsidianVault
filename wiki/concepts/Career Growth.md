## Definition

Career Growth in the context of software engineering refers to the progression through the engineering ladder, from entry level to Staff Engineer and beyond, encompassing scope expansion, compensation growth, and increased influence.

## Key Information

- Career growth in engineering is unique because of the viable IC track — you don't need to become a manager to advance
- The progression follows scope expansion: task → feature → project → multiple projects/product → industry-wide impact
- Compensation grows geometrically, not linearly, at higher levels — the jump from Senior to Staff can be $100K-$200K+ in total compensation
- Career growth requires explicit self-advocacy — you must communicate your ambitions to your manager
- The panel identified three key evaluation areas for career growth: project selection, manager relationship, and team/org choice
- Job hopping is a valid career growth strategy — Zach was only promoted once in his career (Junior to mid-level); every other promotion came from job changes. Zach argues interviewing is the highest-leverage career skill, more important than on-the-job impact. He went from IC3 to IC6 (Staff) in roughly 3-4 years of actual work across Facebook, Netflix, and Airbnb. Career growth requires both learning and earning: if you are not getting at least one of these, leave. Zach notes that after Senior Engineer, technical skills become less important and soft skills (selling vision, deciding which problems to solve, working through others) become the differentiators.
- Pedigree and name brand matter for career growth — being a "Meta Staff Engineer" sets a floor for future opportunities
- Evan King's rapid career growth at Meta: IC3 to IC6 in 3 years, promoted every single year (IC3→IC4 in 1 half, IC4→IC5 in 2 halves, IC5→IC6 in 3 halves)
- Evan's first promotion happened without any self-advocacy — his manager pulled him into a room and told him he was promoted, which was a huge confidence-building moment
- Doing work fast creates a "budget of time" to take on additional scope that accelerates career growth; both Evan and Ryan emphasized this pattern
- The two ways to get more time: optimize your workflow (key bindings, code search, writing down solutions to never solve the same problem twice) or work extra hours (Ryan worked until 9:27 PM shuttles; Evan was passionate enough to wake up in the middle of the night to check model training runs)
- Not getting blocked is critical: after about an hour of research, ask the person who knows — this can save days of work; being able to find the right person and ask effectively is a key soft skill
- Manager advocacy is a critical factor in career growth: Evan's manager proactively created opportunities (tech lead of graphic violence, the Realtime Integrity team) and advocated behind the scenes
- Evan's manager gave him more scope than he could handle and then supported him — this "give them more than they can chew" approach accelerated growth
- The importance of being open to learning from more senior colleagues: Evan initially felt territorial when a senior engineer joined, but self-corrected and built a relationship that became transformative for his growth
- Evan's career path demonstrates that the most impactful solutions are often the simplest: his breakthrough came from using comment signals rather than sophisticated models, improving suicide detection recall from 9% to 55% overnight
- The "next promotion is always right there" can be a trap that keeps you at a company longer than optimal for technical breadth; this is a trade-off between career growth velocity and technical learning diversity
- Ryan felt that going to IC7 would make him a "snowflake" — narrowly useful only at big companies, limiting career optionality. He switched to management partly for broader transferability
- Evan's biggest career regret: not being more inquisitive about how things actually worked, instead just shipping and moving on. Meta's impact-driven culture encouraged this "ship it and run" mentality

- Ricky went from L3 to L6 at Google in 6 years (L3→L4 in 1.5 years, L4→L5 in 1.5 years, L5→L6 in 3 years), roughly 1.5–2 years faster than average at each step
- Accelerated career growth carries trade-offs: Ricky wished he had stayed at the senior level longer to enjoy better Work-Life Balance before the increased meetings and responsibility of staff
- Luck plays a significant role in career growth — Ricky attributes roughly 50% of his success to luck (stable team, good manager, product area he liked) and 50% to conscious preparation
- Asking yourself every ~3 months "am I still enjoying the work?" and "is the grass greener here or elsewhere?" helped Ricky decide to stay at Google for 7 years
- Steve Huynh recommends job hopping every ~2 years until mid-level or senior, then putting roots down for 3-5+ years to reach staff/principal
- Steve's biggest career regret: deferring decisions — staying put by default rather than making an explicit choice about whether to leave
- The default mode is to stay — you need to consciously turn career decisions into active choices rather than passive drift
- Steve emphasized that high-level ICs (Principal, Distinguished) typically have at least 3-5 years on the same team/problem
- At Amazon, the SDE1 to SDE2 promotion is about independence; SDE2 to SDE3 is about ownership; SDE3 to Principal is about cross-team influence
- Steve's advice to his younger self: stop people-pleasing and seeking external validation; examine whether you're climbing the ladder because you want to or because it was put in front of you
- Steve advised asking managers directly: "What are your expectations of me?" and "Am I meeting them?" — most engineers (~75%) never explicitly ask these questions

### Rahul's Career Growth Insights
- Rahul went from L3 to IC6 (Staff) across Pinterest and Meta, with promotions at both
- He was rejected for promotion twice at Pinterest, winning on appeal the second time
- At Meta, he was intentional about not asking for promotion immediately — spent the first year building trust and delivering
- His IC6 promotion came from a Portfolio Approach: one cross-functional infrastructure project and one bottoms-up debug tool
- He recommends job hopping early in career but notes it becomes counterproductive at staff/principal levels where impact comes from relationships and trust
- He advises new grads to take the standard path (big brand name company) unless they have exceptional opportunities and social proof
- Choosing a team that's important to the company accelerates career growth: "a rising tide lifts all boats"
- Having a fast iteration loop is one of the most important things for early-career promotion (L3→L4)
- The manager relationship is critical: Rahul's manager at Pinterest advocated for him in the appeal process
- He advises being explicit about promotion goals but not immediately upon joining — build trust first
- Social proof (Stanford, Facebook internship, big tech offers) enabled his early risk-taking, which accelerated his career
- He believes luck played a large role but can be manufactured through curiosity and relationship-building
- His core advice to his younger self: "There are no gatekeepers" — don't wait for permission to learn, build, or teach

### Philip Su's Career Growth Framework
- Philip Su identifies three main factors for fast career growth: luck, talent, and hard work
- Luck: being at the right place at the right time (e.g., at Stanford when Sergey and Larry were there)
- Talent: not everyone can be an astronaut — "the harsh truth is I am never going to be a great basketball player"
- Hard work: "if you are willing to outwork everybody who is equally talented and equally lucky, you're just going to get further"
- Working longer hours yields diminishing but still positive returns — each additional hour is less effective but total output still increases
- The surgeon analogy: packing six years of experience into four years through more hours makes you a better surgeon coming out
- At Meta, Philip describes the E7 level as regularly impacting the work of ~50 people, able to personally deliver projects on a ~6-month scope, and being regularly consulted on key changes
- At E8/E9, scope multiplies out — both quantitatively (team size) and qualitatively (things no amount of adding people two levels below can replicate)
- Promotions at E8+ are qualitative, not checklist-based: "it's like how you become a professor — at some level it's all the other professors believe you're a professor"
- Philip warns about the "dog that caught the car" — achieving a career goal early with no plan for what comes next, leading to a crisis of purpose
- Philip's advice to his younger self: spend more time clarifying your values before pursuing goals; getting to E7 at 30 vs 38 doesn't matter in the big arc
