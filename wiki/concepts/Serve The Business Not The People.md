## Definition

[[Molly Graham]]'s decision-making mantra for leaders facing emotionally fraught calls (firing, reorganizing, reassigning): the decision that is almost always right is the one that best serves the business, because everyone — the individual included — is ultimately better off if the company succeeds.

## Key Information

- Motivation: leaders who care about being liked, or who have any people-pleasing tendency, get "tangled in the people" during painful decisions (firings, layerings, reorgs), which are emotionally hard both for the affected person and for the manager.
- Practical tool (learned from her own manager): when deciding whether to fire someone or change a project despite knowing it will upset them, ask "if there were no emotions involved — if this person had no negative reaction to this — what would I do?" Then do that thing, and separately optimize *how* you communicate it so the person's pain is as low as possible ("direct is kind").
- Applies to political/interpersonal conflict too: when someone is acting out ("their Bob is raging all over the company" — see [[Bob The Monster]]), remembering that everyone technically shares the same goal (build the biggest, most successful business possible) reframes the disagreement and lets the "people stuff" fall away.
- Explicitly framed as a muscle, not an innate trait — Graham says "these things don't become easy," you just get faster at recognizing when to apply the rule.
- Connects to her closing advice to a colleague at OpenAI ([[Sarah Caldwell]]): amid organizational instability, "the customer is never going to change" — anchoring decisions to what serves the business/customer, not to shifting internal politics, is the stable compass.
