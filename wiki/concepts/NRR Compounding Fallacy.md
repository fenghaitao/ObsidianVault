## Definition

[[Jason Cohen]]'s argument that net revenue retention (NRR), while important, is commonly over-trusted because percentage losses and gains don't cancel out arithmetically — a company can have NRR above 100% while still losing so many logos that not enough customers remain to generate meaningful expansion revenue.

## Key Information

- NRR is computed on the cohort of existing customers only (excludes new customers): revenue from that cohort a year later, accounting for cancellations, downgrades, and upgrades — it can end up above 100% if upgrades exceed losses.
- The compounding-math trap: a 20% loss requires a 25% gain just to get back to even (illustrated via a stock-price analogy: $100 down 20% to $80 does not return to $100 with a 20% gain, only to $96) — so NRR framed simply as "losses offset by gains" understates the real damage of high logo churn.
- Because of the [[Logo Churn Ceiling]], no large public SaaS company can sustain growth with NRR below 100% — Cohen notes only ~2 of 100+ public SaaS companies have NRR under 100%, and those have notably poor financials/valuations; the median NRR at IPO for a SaaS company is 119%.
- Practical implication: growing NRR (via tiered pricing, usage-based expansion, or new features) only works if it's paired with keeping logo churn under control — NRR cannot substitute for fixing a broken retention problem.
- The right question to drive NRR isn't just "what feature can we upsell," but "how do we increase the value the customer is actually getting" (ideally measured, even if only via qualitative proxies), since customers will only pay more if they feel the value increase outpaces the price increase.
- Caution on land-and-expand strategy: pricing "anchors" customers to a reference point — a customer who signed at $10K/year will resist a jump to $100K/year even if usage justifies it, because the increase itself reads as suspicious without a clear, felt value jump to match.
