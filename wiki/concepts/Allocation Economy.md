## Definition

"The allocation economy" is an essay/concept by [[Dan Shipper]] arguing that as AI takes over more execution, human work with AI increasingly resembles being a manager — allocating, checking in on, and correcting AI "reports" — rather than doing the underlying task directly.

## Key Information

- Core analogy: managers are not idle; effective managers spend a lot of time checking in with employees and adjusting course, and Dan Shipper argues the same intensity of attention is required to manage AI agents well, even though there are differences between managing humans and managing models.
- Directly underlies Dan Shipper's recurring claim in this episode that "[[Automation Is A Lie|automation is a lie]]": more automation does not mean less human work, it means the human's work shifts toward allocation, oversight, and course-correction — which is why he says he personally works "way more" despite having far more AI/automation available.
- Connects to why benchmarks (which emphasize autonomy) understate real workload: they capture what a model can do unsupervised in principle, not the ongoing managerial attention needed to make that output actually useful and correct in practice.
