## Definition

Two-Way Door Decisions is a decision-making framework that distinguishes between reversible decisions (two-way doors) and irreversible decisions (one-way doors). Rahul Pandey used this framework to justify taking the risk of joining a startup right out of college instead of going to big tech.

## Key Information

### The Framework
- A two-way door decision is one you can try, learn from, and then back out of if needed
- A one-way door decision is irreversible — once you go through, you can't easily come back
- Most things in your career are two-way doors: you try it out, you learn something, you experiment, and then you can always decide to leave

### Rahul's Application
- Joining the startup Cos was a two-way door: he could try it, and if it didn't work out, he could always go to big tech
- This reasoning was reinforced by his social proof — he had Stanford on his resume and offers from all major tech companies
- He had interned at Facebook and received offers from Google, Facebook, Microsoft, Twitter, and Square
- Without that social proof, he admits he might not have taken the startup risk

### When to Apply
- Early in your career, most decisions are two-way doors because you have less responsibility and more time to recover
- The framework is most useful when you have a safety net (social proof, financial stability, in-demand skills)
- If you lack the safety net, taking the standard path (big brand name company) is the safer two-way door

### Philip Su's Application
- Philip Su switched between IC and management tracks at least six times in his career, keeping the two-way door open by continuing to code while managing
- He warns that many people cannot make the transition back: "they either are unwilling to take a lower level job in order to be an IC, or they are unable to meet the expectations of their level as an IC"
- He deliberately sought a demotion from E9 to E7 when switching back to IC, acknowledging that the skills from being a Site Director were very different from those needed as a coder
- His advice: "be very sensitive to whether or not it locks you into a career you don't want"

### Sash's Application
- Sash framed leaving Meta for startups as a two-way door: "you can always come back and be hired into the next big tech company"
- He was careful but willing to take the risk because of where he was in life — lucky enough to absorb the downside of a failed experiment
