## Definition

[[Molly Graham]]'s signature career/management framework: as a company scales, treat whatever you've become good at building as a pile of Legos you must repeatedly hand off to someone else so you can move on to a bigger, less-defined pile — rather than clinging to the scope you've mastered.

## Key Information

- Origin metaphor: someone dumps a giant pile of Legos in front of kindergarteners and says "build something." It's overwhelming at first, then you build something you're good at (a "house") and want to keep doing it forever — but a scaling company always shows up next and says "it's not a house, it's a neighborhood," handing your half-built house to someone else while you move on to build "dog parks and streets," things that feel entirely unrelated to what you were good at. The cycle repeats: house → neighborhood → city → country → world.
- The emotional core of the framework: giving away scope triggers real, unavoidable feelings — territoriality, fear the new owner will "screw it up," fear you're handing away "all the fun work." Graham says these emotions never fully go away even after 18-20 years of practice, but that doesn't mean you should act on them by holding on.
- Practical urgency: because a scaling company's "Lego pile" (opportunity, headcount, scope) keeps growing however fast the company's growth graph is going, staying attached to your current pile means literally being buried under it. At Facebook, Graham says she reached a point of "giving away my job every 3 weeks" — constantly rehiring herself into new scope.
- The article/talk originated informally: at Facebook, she'd periodically have to sit someone down for "the Legos talk" whenever she saw them start asking territorial questions like "why are we hiring that person?" or "what does that team even do?" — eventually formalized into a widely-read article.
- No fixed rule of thumb exists for when you *shouldn't* give away your Legos; Graham's general stance is "embracing change is far better than fighting it," since you almost never can see what's around the corner, and even outcomes that feel bad in the moment (e.g., being "layered" — having a manager inserted above you) often turn out well in retrospect.
- Explicit reframe offered mid-conversation by Lenny Rachitsky, confirmed by Graham: the Legos were never really yours to begin with — they belong to the company/shareholders — which is part of why holding on is ultimately a losing, "worst version of yourself" strategy.
- Connects directly to her "[[J Curve Versus Stairs]]" framework (giving away the Legos is what frees you up to jump onto the next J-curve) and to her broader thesis that "what you know today is way less valuable than what you can learn by tomorrow" — she notes Google reportedly rewrote its entire codebase every eight years, underscoring that clinging to current skills/scope makes you "irrelevant and extinct."
