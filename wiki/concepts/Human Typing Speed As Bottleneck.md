## Definition

[[Alexander Imbiricos]]'s (Codex product lead, [[OpenAI]]) central answer to "how far are we from AGI": the current limiting factor on AI's real-world economic impact is not model capability or compute, but literal human typing/multitasking speed — humans still have to write prompts and review agent output, which caps how much value even a highly capable agent can deliver per day.

## Key Information

- Framing: users today prompt AI perhaps "tens of times" per day, while a genuinely intelligent, always-available agent could plausibly be useful "thousands of times" per day — the gap between those two numbers is bounded by human input/review bandwidth, not the model.
- Directly tied to why reviewing AI-written code (not writing it) has become the less-fun, bottlenecking part of software engineering, and why [[Codex]]'s product roadmap increasingly focuses on validation/review UX rather than only code generation.
- Prediction built on this framing: a staggered "hockey stick" of productivity gains — early-adopter startups building on agent-native stacks next year, larger and more legacy-encumbered companies (his example: SAP-style enterprises) over the following years as they replace or update systems to let agents work more self-sufficiently, and AI labs' own internal productivity accelerating last of all. That final inflection — when hockey-stick gains flow back into the AI labs themselves — is roughly Imbiricos's operational definition of reaching "the AGI tier."
- Connects to his "[[Chatter Driven Development]]" thought experiment and a cited real-world example from [[Dhanji Prasanna]]/[[Goose]] at Block: even when an agent can act proactively based on ambient signals, the remaining human bottleneck is validating that its actions were correct.
