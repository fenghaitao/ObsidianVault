## Definition

Core principle behind [[Dr. Becky Kennedy]]'s "good inside" idea — that a person's identity (they are fundamentally good) must be treated as separate from their behavior (which may be problematic), because collapsing the two together is what makes difficult conversations unproductive and defensive.

## Key Information

- Kennedy is explicit this is not an excuse for bad behavior: "no part of me, if my kid is hitting, would say my kid is good inside so who cares that they hit — or this employee is good inside so it doesn't matter that they're not getting their PRDs in on time." The behavior still needs addressing; the point is *how*.
- Most interpersonal difficulty comes from inferring identity from behavior — "someone's late to work a lot: oh, that person's lazy" — which she reframes as "this is a good person who is late" by deliberately holding identity (one hand) and behavior (the other hand) as physically/conceptually separate.
- Mechanism for defensiveness: people become defensive because they hear an identity accusation ("you're lazy/a bad person") inside what was meant as a behavior comment ("you're often late"), which shuts down the actual behavioral conversation entirely — "the quickest way to have an unproductive conversation is to lose sight of the fact that someone's good inside."
- Practical script for a chronically late colleague: open by naming you're on the same team and that you know they're a good person, before naming the specific behavior pattern and inviting them to explain what's going on (job stress, home issues, time-management difficulty) — modeled on how a good sports coach addresses a slumping player ("you're a good player, something's going on, let's get in the gym and figure it out") rather than attacking their character.
- The [[Most Generous Interpretation]] (MGI) is the actionable tool Kennedy built to operationalize this principle in the moment, after finding "people are good inside" too theoretical on its own to act on.
- Extended to adults more broadly: Kennedy argues kids (and, by extension, difficult coworkers) who act out badly aren't taking advantage of leniency — they're often signaling a wish for someone ("an adult in the room") to notice the acting-out and help, not confirmation that limits don't matter.
