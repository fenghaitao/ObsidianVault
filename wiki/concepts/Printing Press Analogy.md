## Definition

[[Boris Cherny]]'s preferred historical analogy for the current AI moment: like the printing press, AI is taking a skill locked away in a small specialized class (literacy then; programming/building software now) and making it broadly accessible, with disruptive short-term costs and unpredictable long-term upside.

## Key Information

- Historical detail: in mid-1400s Europe, literacy was below 1%, largely confined to scribes employed by (often illiterate) lords and kings. In the 50 years after Gutenberg's press, more printed material was produced than in the prior thousand years, and printing costs fell roughly 100x. Literacy itself took much longer to spread (~200 years to reach ~70% globally), gated by the need for education systems and free time.
- A period interview with a scribe (cited by Cherny) found the scribe was excited about the press, not threatened: what he didn't enjoy was copying text between books, and he was glad his freed-up time could go toward the parts he loved (illustration, bookbinding) — Cherny draws a direct personal parallel: he doesn't miss the tedious mechanics of coding, and is freed to focus on figuring out what to build, talking to users, and thinking about systems and the future.
- Cherny's optimistic framing: broad literacy was a precondition for the Renaissance's spread of knowledge; he expects a similarly unimaginable-in-advance set of consequences once "everyone can program" the way everyone learned to read and write — while acknowledging the transition will be genuinely disruptive and painful for many in the near term, a societal conversation he says the industry has a responsibility to have openly.
