## Definition

Malleable software, as articulated by [[Max Schoening]] (Head of Product, [[Notion]]), is the idea that software should work closer to the interests of the people who use it than the interests of the corporation that made it — i.e., users should be able to rearrange, adapt, and tweak the tools they depend on the way they would rearrange furniture in their own home, rather than accept an app's behavior as fixed by a vendor's design decisions.

## Key Information

- Framing quote: "Malleable software is the idea that software works closer to the interest of the people that use it than the interest of the corporation that makes it." Schoening contrasts this with living in a home where "you do not get to rearrange your living room" because someone else decided the layout — a constraint we would never accept physically but routinely accept in software.
- Diagnosis of today's app ecosystem: apps bundle UI, data ownership, and behavior into one inflexible "little square on your phone" — if you love 90% of an app but want to change a little of its behavior, that's usually not possible.
- The two existing "solutions" and why both fall short: (1) running your own Linux distribution gives malleability but at the cost of constant DIY maintenance (e.g., "why doesn't the trackpad work"), and (2) most commercial software gives you none. Schoening frames the goal as computing ownership without regressing on collaboration, security, or convenience.
- Cites [[Ink & Switch]] and collaborator [[Jeffrey Litt]] (Schoening: "I get to work with Jeffrey Litt every single day") as people who've spent a long time researching how to make software more malleable while preserving real-time collaboration and security — i.e., malleability built on a shared platform/OS, not just individual bespoke tools.
- Distinguishes malleable software from purely individual tool-building: malleability only scales if it's built on a platform that "encourages this," otherwise everyone ends up with disconnected personal tools rather than shared, communal ones.
- Connected explicitly to the "SaaS apocalypse" debate: Schoening thinks general-purpose tools (word processors, spreadsheets, Notion itself) will become progressively more malleable via AI, while specialized as-a-service tools (security, compliance) remain vendor-maintained — see [[SaaS Apocalypse]].
- Tied to Notion's own AI agent success: Schoening frames Notion as "an operating system" more than an app, which is why AI agents roaming a connected, permissioned workspace resemble coding agents operating in a Unix-like environment — malleability and agent-friendliness reinforce each other.
- Schoening credits Lenny Rachitsky's own behavior (building personal tools for podcast prep/recording) as an example of someone "awakening" to malleable software without necessarily naming it that way.
- Cross-references the "timeless way of building" / Christopher Alexander-style architecture idea (via [[Stewart Brand]]'s *How Buildings Learn*): the best-fitting homes (or software) are often not those designed top-down by an expert but those that adapt slowly, over time, to how their actual occupants live.
