## Definition

Technical Breadth vs Depth describes the trade-off between being a generalist who can work across the full stack (breadth) and a specialist who has deep expertise in a narrow domain (depth). Big tech tends to develop depth, while startups force breadth.

## Key Information

- Evan's big tech experience: narrow depth — he worked on a specific ML infrastructure subsystem and hadn't coded significantly in 1.5 years as staff
- Evan's startup experience: forced breadth — he had to learn everything from caching layers to databases to frontend development
- "At Meta you work on the narrowest... I work on my small thing and then I hit go and then within six hours that is affecting 3 billion people — but I didn't figure out how to make that happen"
- The depth from big tech is valuable but can be limiting: "I had never set up a simple caching layer, I'd never configured my own database, I had never stood up my own [infrastructure]"
- Ryan suggested the ideal order: startup first (get breadth), then big tech (get depth) — "then the optimizations that you're doing in big tech are within context"
- Evan's regret: not being more inquisitive about how things worked at Meta — "I wish I had been a bit more inquisitive... Facebook publishes all of these different blogs and now that I do so much time learning system design, I read all these blogs now and I wish I had done some of that while I was actually at Meta"
- The "snowflake" problem: too much depth at one company makes you narrowly useful and limits career optionality
- Both breadth and depth are valuable, and the ideal career path probably includes both

### Sash's Breadth-First Career
- Sash switched teams ten times in nine years, scaling breadth across design, hardware, blockchain, and AI rather than going deep in one domain
- His rationale: breadth builds a wide network and a toolbox of skills and connections that compound later, even if each individual bet is high-risk
- The trade-off he accepts: it's easier to level in the same space where goodwill and expertise accrue, but that felt "boring" to him
- His boot camp cohort who stayed put grew vertically into managers or deep IC roles; Sash preferred spreading across the org

### David Fowler's Architect Breadth
- Microsoft's "architect" archetype is breadth over depth: overseeing the design of the whole system (web framework, server, packaging)
- At top levels there are several valid archetypes — super coders, deep experts (a GC guru), and area overseers — and you choose which kind of impact suits you

### Bobby Holley's Guided Breadth
- Holly jumped to whatever felt like the biggest, gnarliest problem (DOM bindings, media, multiprocess, Quantum CSS, WebRender, mobile)
- That produced prolific, broad codebase knowledge — the traditional Mozilla DE archetype — even though the criteria later shifted toward industry impact

### Adam Ernst's Depth-First Breadth

- Stayed on iOS/mobile his whole career ("that's where things have taken me"), building "deep knowledge expertise about how all different parts of our system work."
- Built breadth organically: when a problem blocks him he dives "eight levels deep" into that system's guts (GraphQL codegen, Buck) and either fixes it himself or shows up informed — learning new systems only when they come up.
- Says he'd maybe pick AI over mobile if starting today, but doesn't regret the depth.
