## Definition

[[Jason Cohen]]'s argument that standard microeconomic demand-curve intuition (raising price always reduces signups) is usually wrong in practice for early-stage SaaS, because price doesn't just filter *how many* buyers you get — it determines *which market segment* you're selling to, and a too-low price can actively repel the segment that would value the product most.

## Key Information

- Most early/growing companies have simply guessed their pricing and never revisited it — per [[Patrick Campbell]]'s line: "your prices are way too low because you just guessed and you haven't changed them."
- Counterintuitive but common outcome: raising prices (even dramatically, e.g. 12x) often doesn't reduce signups at all, and can increase them, because a very cheap price signals low quality/seriousness to larger buyers ("if it's only $100/month, it can't be good enough for us") who then don't even consider the product — they were never in the addressable market at that price.
- The real shape of the demand curve, per Cohen, is more like a "mesa" (rises, plateaus, then eventually falls at a much higher price point) than the textbook downward-sloping line — only the very lowest-value segment of the market behaves according to classic demand-curve intuition.
- Price is not just the number on the page — structure, framing, and positioning matter just as much. Cohen's "Double Down" thought experiment: a product that halves a customer's $40K/month ad spend could be priced as "saves you money" (worth ~$5K/month to the customer) or repositioned as "doubles your leads for the same budget" (worth the full $40K) — same product, 8x the revenue, purely from what the pricing is framed as delivering.
- Practical lesson for product/pricing decisions: sell more of what the buyer's organization already values (e.g., growth, market share, competitiveness) rather than efficiency/cost-savings framing, since savings-framed value is capped much lower than growth-framed value even when the underlying product is identical.
- Caution: pricing isn't an isolated dial — moving upmarket via price changes what the product needs to include (compliance, integrations, professional services, governance) and can conflict with company culture/goals (Cohen cites Buffer's deliberate choice to stay serving individuals/small teams rather than move upmarket).
- Root philosophy behind pricing decisions, per Cohen: figure out how to genuinely create more value for the customer, then decide how to split that value between customer and company via price — not just treat price as a lever to pull independently of value delivered.
