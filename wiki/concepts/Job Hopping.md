## Definition

Job Hopping is the strategy of changing companies to achieve higher levels, compensation, and career growth faster than internal promotion would allow. It is closely related to Company Hopping and is Zach Wilson's primary career acceleration strategy.

## Key Information

- Zach achieved two of his three career promotions through job hopping: IC4-to-IC5 (Senior) by moving from Facebook to Netflix, and IC5-to-IC6 (Staff) by moving from Netflix to Airbnb
- Job hopping works because new companies only see your impact story, not your hours worked, sustainability concerns, or negative internal data
- The strategy requires a compelling impact story that sells you as already operating at the target level
- Zach leveraged competing offers from Facebook, Google, and Airbnb to negotiate a Staff title at Airbnb — the job description required 10+ years of experience and he had 6
- Job hopping requires countering the "disloyal job hopper" stigma early in interviews by presenting yourself as "extremely ambitious" who "solves hard problems and is on a trajectory to help us solve hard problems"
- Zach's viral LinkedIn post: "apply for jobs that you don't think you're ready for"
- The strategy is especially effective when internal promotion is blocked by factors like manager changes, org restructures, or sustainability concerns
- Job hopping carries trade-offs: multiple 401ks to merge, health insurance changes, rebuilding internal brand at each new company
- Steve Huynh recommends job hopping every ~2 years until mid-level or senior, then putting roots down for 3-5+ years to reach staff/principal
- Steve argues that at the highest levels (Principal, Distinguished), you won't find engineers who haven't been on the same team/problem for at least 3-5 years
- The min-max strategy: job hop every 2 years until mid-level, then stay put to go deep if you want to become a high-level IC
- Steve stayed at Amazon for 18 years because he was able to change teams and products internally, effectively having "many careers" at one company

### Rahul's Perspective on Job Hopping
- Rahul got a level promotion by job hopping from Pinterest to Meta (IC4 to IC5/senior)
- He believes job hopping is more effective early in career — "as long as you show competency, that might be enough for a company to bring you in at a higher level or higher compensation"
- Later in career, job hopping is "actually counterproductive" because impact at staff/principal levels comes from relationships and trust built over time
- If you're a staff or principal engineer, it's very hard to job hop your way beyond that — you need to build trust and relationships to get promoted
- Early on, job hopping works because companies can assess your competency directly; later, your impact is through influence on others, which requires tenure
- Rahul's Meta move was also motivated by Meta being a much bigger company with a stronger engineering brand, more pay, and clearer career growth pathway

### Stefan Mai's Perspective
- Early-career moves are growth — an unfamiliar environment forces you to generalize your skills
- Frequent senior-level moves are a red flag: a hiring manager is betting a year of ramp-up on someone likely to leave in six months
- The credibility you accumulate by staying (the snowball) is powerful, but it can become a shackle if you get stuck as "the person who built that system"

### Snapchat-director guest's View
- He never considered job hopping the only or best way to level up — peers stayed at Facebook and reached VP — but he explains his own moves as deliberate steps toward a north star (CTO of an AI company)
- He never proactively searched for jobs; opportunities came inbound, and he preferred roles where he had a personal connection
- He took a director offer at Square (rather than being promoted into it) and later intentionally chose unfamiliar domains (e.g., Director of Camera at Snapchat) to fill gaps on the way to his goal

### UCLA Talk: Job Hopping Strategy by Level
- Ryan presented a nuanced view: job hopping for promotions is optimal when you're a junior/mid-level/senior trying to get promoted — "aggressively try to job hop" and take the faster path because momentum doesn't matter as much
- Once you reach staff (E6/L6) and above, job hopping becomes "counterproductive" — no one hires at E7/L7; if you're an L6, companies will down-level you
- At staff+ levels, promotions come from building credibility, trust, and a track record over time — job hopping destroys that
- A rare edge case: if you leave the ladder (e.g., start your own company), you enter a "probability cloud" where no one knows your level, and you can potentially come back at a higher level if the work matches
- Ryan cited a rare example of someone going from E5 to E7 by leaving Google, joining a startup, writing a JavaScript book, and returning at E7
