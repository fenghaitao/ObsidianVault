## Definition

Scope refers to the breadth and depth of impact an engineer has, progressing from a single task at entry level to industry-wide influence at the distinguished level. Getting enough scope is the primary challenge for Staff Engineer promotion.

## Key Information

- Scope progression: entry level (task) → mid-level (feature) → senior (project) → staff (multiple projects or whole product) → principal/distinguished (industry-wide impact)
- Not all problems are created equal — working on menial bug fixes will never get you promoted
- A big part of being a Staff Engineer is getting enough scope to justify the promotion
- Senior engineers develop a "muscle" to determine how much impact a project has before investing time
- This muscle is developed by looking at org incentives, published team plans, and metrics
- The team you're on heavily determines available scope — flagship products offer more scope than niche products
- Infrastructure teams tend to offer more natural scope because the technology itself multiplies across many engineers
- Product teams require leading large teams of engineers for scope, and there are fewer such spots
- You can sometimes create scope by finding cross-team impact opportunities even on a less prominent product

- Ricky's L4→L5 transition was driven by becoming the go-to person for a specific scope area in Google ads, which he chose because it "didn't look that pretty" and he wanted to make it better
- His L5→L6 transition involved pushing the boundaries of that scope — challenging the status quo and having larger influence over the area rather than just knowing it well

### Philip Su's Scope Definition
- Philip defines scope as "how big or how long or how valuable of a project can you single-handedly guarantee the delivery of"
- An intern can't even guarantee delivery of a feature; a senior (E5/E6) should deliver the work of ~10-15 people; E7 should regularly impact the work of ~50 people and deliver projects on a ~6-month scope
- At E8/E9, scope multiplies both quantitatively (influencing ~100 people) and qualitatively (things no amount of adding people two levels below can replicate)
- There are two paths to large scope: deep narrow specialists (can do things others can't) and broad generalists (jack of all trades, especially valuable for nascent teams)
- Philip's E9 promotion was driven by strategic scope: decisions that impacted 18 months into the future (e.g., intern hiring based on H-1B projections)
- Strategic scope at senior levels involves thinking about things like the "pyramid of seniority" — whether the right mix of seniority exists and whether people are growing at commensurate velocity
