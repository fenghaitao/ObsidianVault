## Definition

Principle articulated by [[Mark Pincus]] distinguishing "belief" (confidence grounded in evidence/lived experience with the product and its users) from "hope" (confidence without basis — "a prayer"). Teams should kill ideas sustained only by hope as early as possible, rather than let hope for the next release keep them going.

## Key Information

- "The best product makers... they're collecting winnings. They're not making bets" — e.g., [[Brian Chesky]] "already knows he has a hit" before launching, rather than launching to find out.
- Introduces the distinction between a **Minimum Viable Product** (MVP) — built from *hope* that it might be viable — and a "**[[Maximum Launchable Product]]**," built from *belief*, based on evidence, that it will succeed.
- AI's danger: it makes it cheap and fast to reach a "viable" product (months instead of years), which risks reinforcing hope-driven bets rather than being used to run many fast, cheap failure tests. Pincus argues AI should instead be used to build "100 ideas in a day" rather than one polished idea over three months — a "testing machine, failure machine."
- Guiding question: "What can I believe today?" If the honest answer is "this is the wrong product," don't spend three months building it — spend a day or a week building just enough to get signal.
- Practical example: Zynga's FarmVille expansion-pack ad-budget test (turning a coming-soon ad line item into an in-game, on-board test) generated real product/marketing signal instead of hope-based advertising spend, and unexpectedly produced $19M in early-access key sales.
- Related to knowing when an idea is a "B+" rather than an "A": Pincus argues the tell is the same as knowing you're with the right romantic partner — if you have to ask whether it's an A, it isn't one. "When you have lightning in a bottle, when you have true signal, everything works."
