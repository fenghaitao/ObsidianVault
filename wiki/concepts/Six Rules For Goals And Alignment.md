## Definition

[[Molly Graham]]'s six rules for setting goals that actually create organizational clarity and alignment, developed largely from her time at [[Facebook]]. Her starting premise: "goals are a communication tool" whose entire purpose is to tell someone sitting down at their desk what the most important thing to work on is — not a compliance exercise, and definitely not an OKR spreadsheet with a hundred lines "written in Greek."

## Key Information

Two framing points before the six rules:
- Goal-setting cadence should match company stage: a seed-stage company should set goals every ~2 months in a highly iterative way; an established business with a clear go-to-market motion can set annual goals. Annual goals are "a waste of time" for early-stage companies.
- She has "a bone to pick with OKRs" as commonly practiced — not because the framework is bad, but because in practice it frequently produces goal documents so dense ("a hundred lines... written in Greek") that they create zero clarity for the people meant to act on them.

The six rules:
1. **No company needs more than three goals.** Facebook ran on exactly three for the five years Graham was there: growth (monthly active users, MAU), engagement (how often people came back), and revenue.
2. **One goal has to win in a fight.** When goals conflict (e.g., you could grow MAU cheaply by buying bot accounts, which wouldn't help engagement), the team needs to know, unambiguously, which goal takes priority when trade-offs arise — at Facebook, engagement always won because it was what actually drove revenue and the "heart" of the product.
3. **The "explain it to me like I'm five" test.** An intern who started on Monday should be able to look at the goals and understand them — numbers have to make sense to average people, acronyms have to be explained, or the goals fail as a communication tool.
4. **"[[Strategy Should Hurt]]"** — a phrase Graham borrowed from [[Claire Hughes Johnson]] (author of *Scaling People*): if your goal-setting process isn't painful — if you're not cutting things off the list — you haven't actually prioritized. Founders who can't cut down a 10-goal list are implicitly letting employees prioritize for them, since people will show up and work on *something* every day regardless.
5. **One goal has exactly one owner.** "Two people owning a goal is no one owning a goal." The owner should never be the CEO — it should be a specific named person who is accountable, "come hell or high water," for that number. This is deliberately painful organizational work (deciding who owns what), but necessary; it connects directly to the "[[Waterline Model]]"'s structural layer.
6. **Goals by themselves are not enough.** You need a process to follow up on goals, hold people accountable, and — especially early in a company's life — *learn* from whether or how hard it was to move the number. Graham cites [[James Clear]] (author of *Atomic Habits*): "winners and losers have the same goal."
