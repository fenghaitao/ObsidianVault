## Definition

Negotiation principle (popularized by [[Chris Voss]]) [[Jacob Warwick]] repeatedly applies: don't reflexively accept a middle-ground compromise just because it feels fair or easy — the "midpoint" between two numbers is often an arbitrary artifact of insufficiently aggressive anchoring, not a true measure of value.

## Key Information

- Severance case study: a client's agreed "6 months on-target earnings" severance was rewritten in paperwork as "6 months base salary" (roughly half the value). The client was about to informally split the difference (settling ~$260K) rather than simply asking "was that a mistake?" — which got the full original terms restored, saving ~$90,000+.
- Hollywood writer case study: a studio offered $700K; attorneys countered $1.3M; the deal settled at exactly the midpoint ($1M) — Warwick calls this "lazy negotiating," arguing the attorneys never actually found the real ceiling, since a higher counter-anchor (e.g. $1.7M) would very plausibly have settled higher too.
- General principle: the party that anchors more aggressively wins disproportionately more, since counterparties tend to split the difference from wherever the anchor is set — meaning under-anchoring (out of a desire not to seem greedy) directly and predictably costs money.
- Complements Warwick's broader advice to slow down and ask clarifying questions ("was that a mistake?" / "can you help me understand?") before accepting any number, rather than settling out of social discomfort.
