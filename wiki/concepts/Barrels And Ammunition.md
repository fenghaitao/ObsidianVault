## Definition

[[Keith Rabois]]'s framework: within any organization, only a small number of people ("barrels") can independently drive an initiative from inception to success; everyone else is "ammunition" that barrels can deploy. Hiring more people without increasing the number of barrels just stacks people behind the same initiatives, increasing coordination/collaboration tax without increasing throughput.

## Key Information

- Rabois developed this after repeatedly hearing frustrated CEOs (at dinners, in one-on-ones) complain that despite raising money and hiring a lot of people, less was getting done per unit time, not more.
- Concrete calibration: at [[PayPal]] (~254 people in Mountain View), Rabois estimates there were 12-17 barrels — an unusually high ratio credited with the "PayPal Mafia" effect. He contrasts this with a large company where a founder reported only two barrels total.
- A "barrel" is defined behaviorally: given an outcome (e.g., "get us over that hill"), a barrel will motivate people, accumulate resources, and deliver the outcome — or proactively surface blockers, a diagnosis, and what's already been tried, with enough lead time for the CEO to help. Illustrated by Rabois's "smoothie test" hiring story at Square (an intern who, unprompted, solved a persistent late-night-snack logistics problem within a day).
- The ratio of barrels to ammunition, not headcount alone, determines how many important initiatives a company can pursue in parallel or sequence — implying deliberate team construction should start by counting barrels, then sizing ammunition to the problem.
- Complements [[Undiscovered Talent]]: since barrels are rare and expensive to identify externally, much of the skill of scaling an organization is finding overlooked, undiscovered barrels rather than only hiring proven ones.
