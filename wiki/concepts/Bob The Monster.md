## Definition

[[Molly Graham]]'s technique for handling the emotional turbulence of organizational change: externalize the anger/fear/impostor-syndrome reactions that come with scaling, reorgs, and "[[Give Away Your Legos|giving away your Legos]]" into a named, personified "monster" (hers is named Bob), whose sole job is to make you the worst version of yourself — so you can notice his voice without obeying it.

## Key Information

- Graham says she "was raised on the Muppets" and thinks in weird animal metaphors; Bob is the name she gave to the composite of territorial, defensive, panicked feelings that surface during change ("that person took all the fun Legos, you should go push them over and grab them back," "you should send the rage email at 9pm and burn the house down").
- Core rule: Bob's emotions are normal but not useful — "they are not the compass that should be telling you what to do." The goal isn't to eliminate the feelings (they never fully go away, "even after 18-20 years") but to let them pass without acting on them.
- Timing rule of thumb: give any Bob-triggered reaction **two weeks**. Emotions from a specific trigger (a new hire, a layering, a reorg) typically fade within a couple of days once you're through the initial wave; if a reaction is still present after two weeks, that's a signal it's *not* just Bob — it's something real worth raising with a manager, coach, or friend.
- Also used to reframe the impostor-syndrome experience of leadership: when a leader feels "who even gave me this job?" or fears "everyone's going to see through me," that's Bob — normal, but not evidence of anything true, and not a reason to avoid saying "I don't know."
- Explicitly framed by Lenny Rachitsky as a very Buddhist non-attachment idea ("just don't cling"), which Graham agrees with.
