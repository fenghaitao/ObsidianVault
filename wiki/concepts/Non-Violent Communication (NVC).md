## Definition

Non-Violent Communication (NVC), originated by Marshall Rosenberg and taught by executive coach [[Rachel Lockett]], is a four-step framework for having difficult conversations without triggering defensiveness: observations, feelings, needs, and a request.

## Key Information

- **Observations**: state a plain fact with no interpretation — something you could "take a picture of" and no one would argue with (e.g., "in the last three sprint-planning meetings, I wasn't included").
- **Feelings**: express a genuine emotion, not a disguised accusation. Lockett's key distinction: "I feel like you're being a jerk" is not a feeling — a real feeling is a bodily-rooted emotion word (anxious, confused, hurt), and if "I feel like/that..." is followed by a fact rather than an emotion, it's not actually NVC.
- **Needs**: name the underlying universal human need that isn't being met (clarity, collaboration, connection) — without requiring anything of the other person yet.
- **Request**: a small, genuinely achievable "olive branch" ask — not a demand, and not something the other person is obligated to grant; they may counter-propose instead. The point of the request is to give the other person a low-stakes way to meet and see you, not to force compliance.
- **Core reframe — mutual understanding, not victory**: the goal of any conflict is not to convince the other person that what they're doing is wrong, but to help them understand your experience clearly enough that they can empathize and potentially meet your needs. People typically enter conflict "armored, ready to prove their point," which NVC is designed to counteract.
- **The "net" metaphor** (parallel framing Lockett cites from [[Carole Robin]]'s Stanford "Touchy Feely" course): you can be bold and share your own feelings/reactions on your own side of the net, but the moment you cross it into blaming or assuming things about the other person, they become defensive.
- **Take 100% responsibility first**: before using NVC, Lockett recommends entering with humility — asking what your own contribution to the conflict has been — citing [[Jerry Colonna]]'s line ("how are you complicit in creating the conditions you claim you don't want?") and the "100% responsibility" concept from *[[The 15 Commitments of Conscious Leadership]]*.
- Demonstrated live on [[Lenny Rachitsky]] as a hypothetical: Lockett modeled how she'd use the framework with her own husband about uneven parenting division.
- Reframes conflict itself as valuable: internal ambivalence or interpersonal tension signals something you care about deeply and have to learn from — leaning into it (rather than avoiding it) builds both the relationship and a durable skill.
