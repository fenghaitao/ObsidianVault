## Definition

[[Qasar Younis]]'s explanation for why viral videos of Chinese humanoid robots wielding nunchucks provoke public fear even though functionally similar (and far more advanced) robots have safely worked on car factory assembly lines for 25 years: people fear the *unfamiliar* gap between a machine's programming and its behavior, not the machine's actual capability or danger.

## Key Information

- The nunchuck-wielding robots are real (not faked) but are pre-programmed, cost roughly $15 million to produce, and are not remotely autonomous or sentient — yet viewers' brains fill the gap between "motors following a script" and "a being acting of its own volition" with anxiety.
- People don't feel equivalent anxiety about industrial welding robots because that gap is familiar and well understood, even though those robots are also just executing programmed instructions, often at higher speed and closer to humans.
- Younis's underlying explanation: human threat-perception evolved for physical dangers ("the rustle in the bush is a snake"), so anything genuinely new to a person's experience gets processed through that same fear circuitry regardless of its actual risk profile.
- His practical remedy mirrors [[Anti-AI Sentiment]]'s "learn the tools" advice from episode 07: understanding a technology closes the gap that anxiety otherwise fills, and substituting the word "technology" for "AI" helps de-emotionalize the reaction.
- Extends the same logic to self-driving vehicles: people fixate on hypothetical autonomous-vehicle harm while normalizing the roughly 30,000 annual US traffic deaths caused by human drivers — a normalization Younis compares to how child labor was once unremarkable before the Industrial Revolution.
