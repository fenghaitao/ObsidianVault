## Definition

[[Molly Graham]]'s rule of thumb for leaders, especially when hiring or reassuring people: never promise outcomes that are actually outside your control (stability, specific titles, "you'll never be laid off/managed over"), because a broken promise demoralizes people faster than almost anything else a leader can do.

## Key Information

- The trap: it's tempting, especially when recruiting, to offer false certainty ("your onboarding will be smooth," "sure, you can be my CMO forever") because it feels good in the moment and helps close the hire — but the leader making the promise usually has no actual control over whether it holds.
- The metaphor, borrowed from [[Claire Hughes Johnson]]: "promises like that are letter bombs that you mail yourself that are going to explode in your face in like a year" — short-term relief for the person you're reassuring today, guaranteed pain for both of you later when circumstances change.
- Graham frames a broken promise of this kind as "no faster way to demoralize high performers" or to end up hiring the wrong people — people need to be told the truth about what's out of the company's control so they can decide, with real information, whether they're comfortable with that uncertainty.
- Corroborating anecdote from Lenny Rachitsky: early in his career he told a frustrated stakeholder "it'll never happen again" (a missed deadline), and was corrected — "you can't promise that, that's not true" — a formative lesson in the same principle.
- Connects to Graham's broader closing advice about leading through the Chan Zuckerberg Initiative and OpenAI-scale change: rather than promising stability, tell people to *expect* instability ("assume you're going to have a new boss in six months") and anchor them instead to what genuinely won't change, like the identity of the customer.
