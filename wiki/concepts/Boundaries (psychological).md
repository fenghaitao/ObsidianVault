## Definition

Dr. Becky Kennedy defines boundaries as: "what you tell someone else you will do. And it requires the other person to do nothing." This contrasts with requests, which require the other person to comply. Most people who say "my kid/colleague doesn't respect my boundaries" are actually making requests, not setting boundaries.

## Key Information

- "Boundaries are what you tell someone else you will do. And it requires the other person to do nothing."
- "If you're setting what you call a boundary and you're really making a request, you're giving all of your power away to the other person."
- Request: "Don't press the buttons." → Boundary: "When we go into the elevator, I'm going to stand between you and the buttons, sweetie, because I'm not going to let you press the buttons."
- The boundary-setter must be prepared for emotional fallout: "I always tell parents, I would bet on you any day over your kid."
- When a kid tantrums after a boundary, that's actually a sign of success: "If you actually see that as a sign that you're successfully setting a boundary, instead of as a sign you did something wrong, your relationship with the tantrum changes."
- "I'm oddly sickly grateful for a sign that I set a boundary. You're like, 'Oh, this is actually going according to plan.'"
- At work: "Don't press the buttons" = request; "I'm going to block you from pressing the buttons" = boundary
- "Job confusion" happens when we ask our kid (or subordinate) to do our job because we don't want to deal with emotional fallout
- Threats ("if you press the buttons, no dessert") undermine authority because they give power to the other person
- The boundary setter must be prepared for the person to protest, especially when boundaries are new
