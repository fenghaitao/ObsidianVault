## Definition

A term [[Stewart Butterfield]] credits to Jeff Bezos: the "good enough" bar for any given product dimension (login, checkout, search, speed) keeps rising over time as adjacent and competing software improves, meaning a feature can silently fall out of date even if it never changes.

## Key Information

- Companion concept to Butterfield's "[[Utility Curves]]": the threshold a feature must cross to deliver real value isn't fixed — it moves upward continuously, driven by the general improvement of software people use elsewhere.
- Consequence: most product surfaces get implemented once and then improved only rarely (or never), even as the surrounding bar keeps rising — meaning previously "good enough" experiences quietly become substandard.
- Illustrated with an extended example: Google Calendar's iOS time-zone picker, which presents all time zones in strict alphabetical order (by country name) rather than by relevance to the user's likely needs — an area Butterfield estimates has likely gone unimproved for years, at a company using it internally at massive scale.
- Also connects to Butterfield's public "we should be humiliated" quote about Slack in its first year, which he intended as a standing challenge to keep raising internal standards rather than settling once something initially works.
