## Definition

[[Dr. Becky Kennedy]]'s distinction between a true boundary — "what you tell someone else you will do," requiring nothing of the other person — and a request, which depends on the other person's compliance and therefore isn't a boundary at all, however it's phrased.

## Key Information

- Kennedy's diagnostic test: does the statement say what *I* will do, and does it require the other person to do nothing? If success depends on the other person complying, it was a request, not a boundary — regardless of how firmly it's stated or what consequence is attached.
- Common failure mode she cites: "my kid/colleague doesn't respect my boundaries" almost always describes a request mislabeled as a boundary — e.g., "don't press the elevator buttons or no dessert" is a request-plus-threat, not a boundary, because it still depends on the child's compliance and gives away the parent's authority/power to the other person.
- A real boundary in the same scenario: "I'm going to stand between you and the buttons so you can't press them" — the parent controls the outcome regardless of what the child does, and can simultaneously validate the child's feelings ("I know you wish you could press those") without abandoning the boundary.
- Kennedy frames setting a boundary this way as more effective than threats specifically because threats (e.g., "no dessert") tend not to be followed through, which undermines authority far more than the original unwanted behavior did.
- One of what Kennedy calls the "two jobs" of any sturdy leader (see [[Sturdy Leadership]]): setting boundaries (limits/decisions made from a position of authority or with information the other person doesn't have) and validating the other person's experience without being taken over by it. She explicitly warns that validating feelings alone is "an incomplete parenting approach" — and by extension, an incomplete leadership approach — without also holding boundaries.
- Workplace translation: a leader making a real decision (e.g., a policy change) rather than putting it to a vote is a boundary, not a request for consensus; Kennedy contrasts this with occasions that genuinely call for consensus-building, saying leaders should distinguish which kind of decision they're facing.
- Related concept she names for the failure mode of *not* setting boundaries: "[[Job Confusion]]" — asking the other person to do your job for you (i.e., to spare you the discomfort of holding the line yourself).
- Adults' need for boundaries mirrors "potty learning" for toddlers: kids control very little in early life (mainly what goes in/comes out of their body), which is why those domains become fiercely contested; Kennedy extends this to workplace "resilient rebels" (see [[Resilient Rebels]]) who react strongly to a perceived loss of control over their own domain, and recommends explicitly naming what someone owns/drives versus where a manager will be involved.
