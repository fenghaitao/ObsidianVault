## Definition

[[Qasar Younis]]'s argument that major Chinese tech companies like [[Huawei]] should not be evaluated as ordinary profit-seeking companies comparable to Western firms, but as extensions of the Chinese state — making direct company-to-company comparisons (Huawei vs. Apple, Chinese EV makers vs. [[Rivian]]) fundamentally misleading.

## Key Information

- Cites *[[House of Huawei (book)|House of Huawei]]*: about a quarter of Huawei's couple-hundred-thousand employees are Communist Party members, and the company's name literally translates to "China's ambition" — its goal is not growing profits for shareholders.
- Analogy: it would be like a company called "MAGA" where a quarter of staff belong to a political party whose stated goal is that party's expansion rather than business profit.
- Consequence for competitive framing: instead of "OpenAI vs. DeepSeek" or "Apple vs. Huawei," the more accurate framing is "OpenAI vs. the Chinese government."
- Consequence for the celebrated-Chinese-EV narrative: Chinese EV makers look superior to Western automakers partly because they aren't assessed on the same profit/sustainability basis a company like [[Rivian]] is — Rivian makes comparably interesting products but loses significant money and is valued far lower by public markets as a result.
- Younis is explicit that this doesn't mean China is incompetent, doesn't warrant serious attention, or that the relationship must be antagonistic — only that it isn't a fair one-to-one comparison, a nuance he says rarely reaches the average person's "China bad" mental shorthand.
- Frames open/free markets as historically self-proving over 100+ years, with the live question being how a non-open-market actor like China's state-directed tech sector interacts with that system.
