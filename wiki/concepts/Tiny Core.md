## Definition

"Tiny core" is [[Max Schoening]]'s (Head of Product, [[Notion]]) theory that every truly great product succeeds because of one exceptionally good, narrow capability — not because of the accumulation of many features — and that chasing "one more feature" to rescue a mediocre product "never works."

## Key Information

- Core claim: "All the great products have something tiny that is a superpower. one tiny core that is so exceptionally good... One of the biggest pitfalls is if you get into the loop of if I just add one more thing to the product, it will be finally great. That never works."
- Examples Schoening gives across his own career:
  - [[GitHub]] — the pull request: the idea that anyone can suggest a change and you can see it.
  - [[Heroku]] — `git push heroku master`: turning "code on my computer" into "a live URL" with one command, at a time when deploying apps was hard.
  - Dropbox — the menu-bar sync icon, so reliable at indicating connectivity that it doubled as a de facto internet-connection indicator; Schoening notes Dropbox's mistake was later trying to expand its surface area instead of protecting that one job.
  - [[Notion]] — blocks and slash commands, plus the blend of real-time collaboration.
  - iPhone — multi-touch.
  - Snapchat — the disappearing-photo concept.
- Frames the tiny core as partly luck (you stumble on it) and partly market validation (the market agrees it matters) — not something you can fully engineer in advance.
- Connects to a broader claim that being first doesn't matter as much as being right: cites AirPods (not the first Bluetooth headphones, but got the core interaction right) and [[Anthropic]] (started after [[OpenAI]], less funded, but "did the same thing twice" successfully under [[Dario Amodei]]).
- Internally at Notion this creates tension with feature proliferation: Schoening cites Notion's six overlapping "automation primitives" as an example of organically letting ideas grow, then needing to do the harder work of consolidating them back into one clean, "naked robotic core" idea — even if that means delaying the next feature to reconcile the current ones.
- Schoening explicitly rejects "feature count" as a meaningful success metric, comparing it to bragging about lines of code written or tokens spent — "I would rather have fewer features that are really really good and where the combinatorics let you do everything."
