## Definition

Framework taught by [[Molly Graham]] (referenced via a guest post on [[Lenny Rachitsky]]'s newsletter in episode 18, and explained in full in episode 34): most team performance problems aren't really about a person's competence, but about structural gaps — unclear expectations, undefined success criteria, or overlapping ownership. Graham is explicit that the model itself isn't originally hers ("it's from some business book somewhere") — she first learned it teaching wilderness leadership at [[National Outdoor Leadership School]] and now teaches it inside her community, [[Glue Club]].

## Key Information

### Per episode 18 (Claire Vo) — AI-agent-management analogy
- Applied here as a direct analogy to troubleshooting an underperforming [[Open Claw]] agent: when an agent does the wrong thing, it's rarely "the AI is dumb" — it's almost always that it lacks the right context, tools, or clearly scoped instructions, which is diagnosable by literally inspecting its file system (see [[Soul And Heartbeat]]).
- [[Claire Vo]] and [[Lenny Rachitsky]] extend the analogy: an agent's failure is a clean diagnostic test of the underlying documentation/communication system — if a tireless AI with unlimited resources can't figure out what's expected of it, that reveals a real structural gap that would just as easily trip up a new human hire.

### Per episode 34 (Molly Graham) — full framework
- Core metaphor: a team is a boat trying to reach a destination (its goals) across an ocean whose surface conditions (choppy or calm) determine how hard that is. The "waterline" question is: what's happening *under the water* that's making the crossing harder or easier?
- Four layers, in descending diagnostic order:
  1. **Structural** — goal-setting, vision, roles, expectations: the shared structures that touch every team member.
  2. **Dynamics** — how the team works together: culture, decision-making, conflict resolution.
  3. **Interpersonal** — relationships between two specific people.
  4. **Intrapersonal** — challenges within a single individual.
- Governing rule: "**snorkel before you scuba**" — start diagnosis at the top (structural), not the bottom (the people). Graham estimates ~80% of team problems are actually structural or dynamics issues, even though the default instinct when something's going wrong is to jump straight to blaming or examining specific people.
- Her single highest-leverage prescription: "your only goal as a manager, if you do nothing else, [should be] clear roles and clear expectations" — she says that in nearly every team she's taken over, the root problem turns out to be that no one actually knows what their job is or what success looks like, and fixing just those two things resolves a large share of other visible issues.
- Practical application as a manager: run a two-sided conversation — "here's what I'm seeing, tell me what you're seeing" / "what do you think your job is? what number were you hired to drive?" — because an employee's mental picture of their role is very often different from the manager's ("you described an elephant and they spat out a tiger"). Fixing that mismatch alone often resolves the apparent performance issue. This routinely requires re-explaining roles/expectations many times over ("I've said this 45 times... turns out no one heard you the first 43 times").
- Directly connects to her "[[Six Rules For Goals And Alignment]]" (especially "one goal has one owner" and the "explain it to me like I'm five" test) as the structural-layer fix this model calls for.
