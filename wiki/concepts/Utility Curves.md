## Definition

[[Stewart Butterfield]]'s framework for deciding whether continued investment in a feature or product dimension is worthwhile: an S-curve with effort/cost on the x-axis and value/utility on the y-axis — early effort produces little value, a "magic threshold" produces an outsized jump in value, and further investment past that point has diminishing returns.

## Key Information

- Basic example: a hammer with a handle that breaks under any impact is useless no matter how nice everything else is — quality has to cross a threshold before the product delivers any value at all; beyond a certain point, further quality investment stops mattering.
- Applied to features (not just whole products): Butterfield argues most features are wrongly treated as binary (you either "have" a feature or you don't); the real question is whether you're on the early flat part of the curve (not enough invested to matter), the steep part (crossing the threshold), or the later flat part (diminishing returns, further work not worth it).
- Common failure mode: a team half-invests in a feature, it never crosses the value threshold, users don't adopt or appreciate it, but it's added permanent complexity to the product — worse than either not building it or fully committing to cross the threshold.
- Paired with "[[Divine Discontent]]": the threshold itself isn't fixed — it moves upward over time as competing/adjacent software raises the baseline for what "good enough" looks like, so a feature that once cleared the bar can silently fall back below it.
- Used at Slack as a shared team lens for whether a specific investment area (search quality, checkout, onboarding) had actually reached the point of mattering, or was still pre-threshold and thus not yet worth defending.
