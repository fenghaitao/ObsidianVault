## Definition

Utility curves are a product management framework based on the S-curve, where the horizontal axis represents cost/effort and the vertical axis represents value/convenience/quality. The curve helps teams determine whether a feature hasn't yet reached its "magic threshold" of value, or has already hit the point of diminishing returns.

## Key Information

- **S-curve shape**: flat at the bottom (little value from initial effort), then a steep arc up (enormous value from a small amount of additional effort), then leveling off (diminishing returns)
- **Purpose**: to counter the binary thinking of "we have this feature or we don't" — instead, assess whether enough investment has been made to reach the value threshold
- **Example**: building a hammer — if the handle breaks on any impact, it's useless. Make it a little stronger, still useless. Then suddenly: good, great. After that, further improvements don't matter
- **Example**: making a users table in a database — you've generated zero value so far, but it's a necessary step on the flat part of the curve
- **Common failure mode**: teams add a feature, it's not good enough (still on the flat part), so people don't use it, and the team concludes the feature isn't worth doing — when the real problem was insufficient investment
- **Layered with "divine discontent"**: the curve shifts over time as customer standards rise, requiring continued investment even in features that were once "good enough"
- Works for both specific features and for the overall product (e.g., getting users to understand what Slack does and then reaching "I can't live without it")
