## Definition

[[Sherwin Wu]]'s central metaphor for AI-native engineering, drawn from MIT's 1980s programming textbook *Structure and Interpretation of Computer Programs* (nicknamed "the wizard book"), which frames programmers as wizards, programming languages as incantations, and code as spells that go out and act in the world.

## Key Information

- Wu argues this 1980 metaphor is proving literally true in the current AI wave: with tools like Codex/Cursor, you can now tell the system exactly what you want in plain language and it will "go do it" — an incantation in the fullest sense.
- Extends the metaphor with Disney's *Fantasia* "Sorcerer's Apprentice": Mickey enchants brooms to do his chores, then falls asleep while they run wild and flood everything, until the master sorcerer returns to clean up. Wu sees this as an apt warning: dispatching many parallel AI agents ("20 different Codex threads") is extremely high-leverage, but requires real skill and seniority to avoid the model "going off the rails" unsupervised — you don't want to fully disengage.
- Cited by Wu as evidence that the fundamental challenge of programming — figuring out the right incantation to make the machine do what you want — has persisted across every era of the field, even as the form of the incantation (assembly, high-level languages, natural-language prompts) keeps changing.
- Complements a similar "genie granting wishes" framing shared by a different Lenny's Podcast guest (per Lenny): be very precise about what you wish for, since imprecise wishes have real (sometimes "monkey's paw"-style) side effects.
