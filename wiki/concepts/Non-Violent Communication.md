## Definition

Non-Violent Communication (NVC) is a four-step framework created by Marshall Rosenberg for resolving conflict and creating mutual understanding. The steps are: Observations (facts), Feelings (emotions without blame), Needs (universal human needs), and Requests (a small, achievable olive branch). The goal is not to convince the other person but to create mutual understanding.

## Key Information

- **O — Observations**: Note what is happening factually. Something you could take a picture of that no one would argue with. Example: "In the last three sprint planning meetings, you didn't include me."
- **F — Feelings**: Express your feelings without blame. Use emotion words (sad, anxious, confused), not judgments ("I feel like you're being a jerk" is not a feeling). Feelings are sensations in your body that result in emotions.
- **N — Needs**: State your universal human needs related to the topic. These are not demands on the other person — just helping them understand what needs are not met. Examples: clarity, collaboration, connection.
- **R — Requests**: Make a small, achievable olive branch for the other person to meet you. They don't have to accept — they can make a counter proposal. The key is mutual understanding.
- The goal of any conflict is to create mutual understanding, not to convince the other person they're wrong.
- When people come to conflict, they often come "armored and ready to prove their point." NVC helps disarm this by staying "on your side of the net" — sharing your experience without crossing into blame or assumptions.
- Professionals have feelings. Tech culture pretends work is purely logical, but it's completely emotional. Ignoring feelings leads to unconscious reactivity.
- NVC is akin to the Stanford "Touchy Feely" course framework (Carole Robin) and the concept of "the net."
- If the other person cares about you, knowing how you feel will naturally motivate them to change — you don't need to demand it.
- Rachel Lockett demonstrated NVC with a CEO whose co-founder was constantly undercutting his decisions. The conversation revealed the co-founder was frustrated with how the CEO was spending his time.
- The framework works best when you enter with humility and curiosity about the other person's experience, not rigidity and blame.
