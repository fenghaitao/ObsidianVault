## Definition

A term [[Stewart Butterfield]] popularized (crowd-sourced from a now-deleted Twitter reply, whose author he can't credit): the pattern where the people building/owning a product routinely fail to serve its most basic, obvious use cases for outside users, because they're too close to their own priorities to see the product as a stranger would.

## Key Information

- Original example: restaurant websites (circa ~10 years before this episode) that buried the five things a visitor actually wants — address, phone number, menu, hours, how to make a reservation — behind slow-loading Ken Burns-effect photo carousels and autoplaying music, with the phone number often not even copyable text.
- Butterfield's point: the restaurant owner themselves has certainly visited someone else's restaurant website specifically to find an address or phone number and been frustrated by the same problem — yet doesn't apply that lived frustration to their own site.
- Connected to "[[Comprehension Vs Friction]]": the fix is to deliberately step back and imagine a first-time, distracted, non-invested user encountering the product cold — Butterfield's suggested exercise is to "close your eyes, take a couple deep breaths, pretend you're a regular human being" before reviewing your own product.
- Cited alongside Apple's unlabeled "Sleep" alarm-clock feature as a modern instance of the same underlying pattern: a team deeply invested in a specific capability failing to make it comprehensible to an outside user encountering it without context.
