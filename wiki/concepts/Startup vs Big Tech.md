## Definition

Startup vs Big Tech is a career trade-off between the depth and specialization of large companies and the breadth and generalism of startups. Each offers different learning experiences, compensation structures, and career paths.

## Key Information

- Evan's experience: "I left big tech with a relative ego... and I leave all of that to realize I don't know a damn thing"
- At Meta, Evan worked on "the narrowest" things — he hadn't coded significantly in 1.5 years as staff, and his coding was on a small subsystem
- In startups, Evan had to do everything: set up caching layers, configure databases, stand up infrastructure, write frontend code (from raw jQuery to React)
- "I had never written a line of frontend code" before the startup
- The startup experience was "incredibly stressful, incredibly overwhelming" but ultimately provided "practical skills — you can drop me into any situation and I can figure it out"
- Big tech gets you depth; startups get you breadth
- Ryan's opinion: "the right thing is to do it in the inverse order — start at a startup and then go into big tech" for optimal learning, but "I wouldn't recommend that from a career perspective"
- Evan's financial assessment: startups haven't out-earned big tech yet, but "the value in terms of experiences and purely technical knowledge has far outpaced that of what I would have learned at Meta"
- Evan's projected lifetime earnings are higher because of the skills learned at startups
- At Meta, "I hit go and then within six hours that is affecting 3 billion people" — but Evan didn't build the infrastructure that made that possible
- Evan wished he had been more inquisitive about how Meta's infrastructure worked while he was there
- Ryan noted that big tech IC7+ can make you a "snowflake" with limited transferability
- The middle ground: join a Series B or C startup to get breadth, then consider founding your own

### Rahul's Experience with Startups and Big Tech
- Rahul chose the startup path (Cos) right out of Stanford, despite having offers from all major tech companies
- His reasoning: the startup was a "Silicon Valley unique story" — a two-way door he could back out of, and he had social proof as an insurance policy
- The startup was acquired after 8 months — closer to failure than success, but he learned a lot and got a great story
- He advises most new grads to take the standard path (big brand name company) unless they have exceptional opportunities and social proof
- "The standard path is standard for a reason" — go to Amazon, Google, or another big brand to build credibility
- Even if your end goal is startups, developing engineering fundamentals at a big company while collecting the brand stamp is "one of the highest EV things you could do"
- He later left Meta specifically to start a company, feeling he had the foundation: a co-founder, financial stability, and signal from side projects
- The biggest difference between startup and big tech: you have to manufacture your own structure, urgency, and deadlines — "there's no manager, no boss, no one looking out for you"
- There's much more ambiguity about what to build — product management thinking is something big tech engineers often have no experience with
- You can have an "amazing life" climbing the ladder at big tech — you're in the top 0.1% of the world
- Start a company if you want more control/autonomy and an outlier outcome with a small chance of a huge upside
- Meta's directness and PIP culture was a notable difference from Pinterest's "too nice" culture

### UCLA Talk: Big Tech vs Startups
- At the UCLA talk, when asked "big tech or startups?", nearly the entire room raised their hands for big tech; only a few for startups
- UCLA students overwhelmingly prefer big tech compared to other colleges — startup recruiters said they stopped coming to UCLA because no one wanted to join
- Stanford and Ivy League schools are the startup-oriented colleges; Ryan speculated it's because "they're rich, so they don't need to make money immediately after college"
- Key differences: big tech provides prestige/brand equity (makes getting hired easier), while startups offer first-mover advantage and higher proportional impact
- It's a trade-off between "high expected value versus high variance": big tech has better average outcomes; startups can boom or bust
- "You will never have a moon up or a moon down scenario" in big tech — you'll make a good amount but never "rich rich" ($10M+). At a startup, you could make eight or nine figures if you're a founder or early employee
- Career growth at startups: being early at a moonshot company can fast-track you to director or VP roles that would take decades in big tech
- Ricky chose big tech partly because he's "lazy" and values good work-life balance — he "sold his soul to Google" for a good life
- The speakers recommended big tech for the first few years: lock in the prestige, learn the basics and best practices, then do whatever you want
- Startup learnings vary widely — your role could involve less code and more PM work, depending on what the startup needs
