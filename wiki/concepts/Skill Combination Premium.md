## Definition

[[Marc Andreessen]]'s argument that AI dramatically increases the value of combining skills across domains (e.g., coding + design + product): being good at two things is worth more than double being good at one, and good at three is worth more than triple, because you become a rare "super relevant specialist" in that specific combination.

## Key Information

- Credits [[Scott Adams]]'s own account of *Dilbert*'s success: neither the world's best cartoonist nor the world's best businessperson could have made Dilbert alone — only someone who was both could.
- Credits [[Larry Summers]]'s framing "don't be fungible": a person with only one narrow skill is replaceable; someone with a rare combination of skills is not, because there may be very few others who can do exactly that combination.
- Applied to tech's product/design/engineering "[[Mexican Standoff (PM Design Engineer)|Mexican standoff]]": rather than one discipline "winning," the individuals who become AI-augmented "superpowered" combinations of two or three roles become the most valuable and hardest to replace.
- Cited Hollywood parallel: directors, writers, and actors face the same standoff; the people who can write *and* direct are called "auteurs" and are the field's biggest creative forces — the entertainment-industry analog of the superpowered combination.
- Visualized as a "T-shape" (one deep skill, several shallower ones) extended into an "E-shape" or sideways-"F-shape" (multiple deep skills) as AI makes it newly feasible for one person to go deep in more than one domain at once — a framing [[Jenny Wen]] separately described (episode 23) as the "block-shaped" strong generalist archetype in her [[Design Hiring Archetypes]].
