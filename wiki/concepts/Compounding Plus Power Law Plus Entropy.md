## Definition

A three-part framework used by [[Matt MacInnis]] (CPO, [[Rippling]]) for how to think about sustained business success: outcomes are power-law distributed, systems naturally decay toward disorder (entropy), and the only antidote to that decay is continuously injected leadership energy.

## Key Information

- **Power law**: business (and broader) outcomes are not linear (y = x); they follow power-law distributions, so the top 5-10% of performers don't get marginally more reward, they get 10x or 100x more. Getting to 80-90% of some threshold barely moves the payoff curve — the inflection point is much further out.
- **Entropy**: the second law of thermodynamics, applied to organizations and codebases — systems left alone tend toward disorder (a sock drawer gets messy, potholes form, aircraft require constant maintenance, abandoned cities decay). MacInnis argues every line of code added to a codebase increases its entropy and demands ever more energy to maintain.
- **Energy as the only antidote**: because [[Teams Optimize For Local Comfort|teams will always drift toward optimizing for local comfort over company outcomes]] (not out of malice, but because that's what happens to any human system left unchecked), leaders must inject energy/intensity at every step to fight entropy — "every time you see a bug... every time, not most of the time."
- **Intensity decay by management layer**: MacInnis argues the founder-CEO is the purest, most intense source of ambition in a business, and each successive layer of management below them risks an order-of-magnitude drop-off in intensity; two layers down can mean two orders of magnitude of signal loss, which he calls "dangerous." A leader's job is not to buffer their team from that intensity but to mirror it and let buffering happen elsewhere in the org.
- Illustrated with an internal example at Rippling: CEO [[Parker Conrad]]'s Slack avatar (a generic yellow egg) became shorthand ("Parker Conrad is modeling personal intensity") for demanding immediate, unfiltered engagement with problems rather than scheduling them away.
- MacInnis reports that, counterintuitively, teams react to this demand for perpetual intensity not with exhaustion but relief/invigoration — "what a relief that I get to go be intense," since few people actually want to work for (or be) a "chill boss."
- Directly tied to [[Extraordinary Results Require Extraordinary Effort]] and to his broader argument that leaving any organizational "crack" (through overstaffing, relaxed process, or executive complacency) invites a hungrier competitor to exploit it.
