## Definition

The traditional design process — research, ideation, divergence, convergence, prototyping, and implementation, historically justified by the assumption that implementation is expensive — is discussed by [[Andrew Ambrosino]] as partially obsolete, but not dead in the way it is sometimes claimed to be.

## Key Information

- The classic process (as Ambrosino describes it, referencing a "case study factory" critique of designer hiring/education) treated the process itself, not outcomes, as the guarantor of quality: "if something went through this process ... it would be good," even if nobody ended up using it. Ambrosino says he was never a fan of this version of the process, independent of AI.
- The process was historically predicated on the assumption that "you can really only afford to build once," so the problem and solution space must be exhaustively explored before implementation. Tools like Figma and Origami partially broke this by making interactive prototyping cheap and pulling it earlier into the process.
- Now that implementation itself (not just prototyping) is cheap via AI, that same collapse is happening one level further: fully-built, production-looking artifacts can appear very early in the process, creating a "mismatch" — people see a polished-looking prototype and assume it's ready to ship, when it may still represent an early exploratory stage.
- Ambrosino agrees with a peer's claim ("Jenny," i.e. [[Jenny Wen]], described as head of design at another AI coding company) that the design process, in the sense of a fixed set of tools/steps tied to specific media, is "dead" — but disagrees that structured thinking about what stage of the process you're in is dead. What must persist is being explicit about "are we clear on what this [artifact] is doing" (exploration vs. near-final) even as tools/media blur together.
- Discusses "baby" simplified codebases (e.g., "baby Codex," analogous to "baby Cursor") — dramatically simplified versions of a production app that approximate its interactions and are fast to vibe-code against — as a modern design-process tool for rapidly testing UI/interaction ideas.
- Position on "PRDs are dead, prototypes are in": Ambrosino thinks this is overstated. The right artifact depends on the goal — a document may still be the right medium for establishing product clarity in a vague area, while a prototype is right for stress-testing an interaction pattern. He also notes engineers now often write excessive, low-value documents simply because writing is easy, which is itself a new failure mode.
- Related term used in this discussion: "the primal mark" (raised by the interviewer, attributed to another podcast guest) — the idea that the first concrete artifact anchors all subsequent thinking, which is why prematurely jumping to a polished prototype can bias a team away from a better underlying direction.
