## Definition

Self-Advocacy is the practice of proactively communicating your career goals, promotion aspirations, and accomplishments to your manager and other stakeholders. It is essential for career advancement, as managers cannot read minds and may not know your ambitions.

## Key Information

- "The squeaky wheel gets the grease" — if you want attention, money, or promotion, you must make noise
- You must advocate for yourself and talk about your work and goals
- Not everyone wants a promotion, and managers may not know your ambitions unless you express them
- The framing of self-advocacy matters: "I want to get promoted" on day one is poorly received, but "Can we have a check-in about promotion in three months?" shows aligned priorities
- You can express interest even if you don't think you're ready: "I want to move up but I don't think I'm ready now — I want your perspective on what's missing"
- Being explicit about promotion at every level is better than being passive — ask about the next level even before the current promotion goes through
- High performers have leverage for self-advocacy: managers are more receptive to career growth requests from people who solve their problems
- Self-advocacy can feel uncomfortable or "dirty" for shy or reserved people, but it's non-negotiable
- Evan King's self-advocacy evolved over his career: his first promotion (IC3→IC4) happened without any self-advocacy at all — his manager proactively told him he was promoted. By the time of his IC5 promotion, he was having open conversations with his manager about promotion timelines and gaps. By IC7 pursuit, he was much more actively advocating for himself
- Evan's manager was the one who proactively created opportunities for him (tech lead, Realtime Integrity team), which is less common — most engineers need to drive this themselves
- Ryan Peterman was more self-advocating from the start: he would come to his manager saying "what do I have to do to get promoted, I'm super motivated, what does it even look like at the next level?"
- Ryan noted that his manager was never unsupportive, but wasn't coming to him proactively — Ryan had to drive the conversation
- Evan observed that many of his boot camp classmates didn't have the same promotion growth because they didn't have a manager advocating for them — and they weren't self-advocating either
- The advice to those without a proactive manager: "you really need to self-advocate, you have to have those conversations with your managers — this is when I want to be promoted, this is what I think I need to do, do you agree, where are the gaps?"
- Self-advocacy is an awkward conversation because it's "setting yourself up, it's vulnerable — but you have to have it"
- You want to hear bad news early rather than "come promo day when you're expecting something"
- At the UCLA talk, Ryan emphasized that promotions are decided by people who need to know about your work. Self-advocacy is the "last few% of your time" after doing great work — you either write about it, present it, or tell your manager. The "tree falling in a forest" analogy: if you do great work and nobody knows, it doesn't matter. You can also get visibility through your manager or PM.

- Ricky practiced self-advocacy through ongoing conversations with his manager, asking "Do you feel like these projects that I'm currently doing are for the next level?" and creating a one-year roadmap for manager buy-in a full year before his promotion
- He explicitly endorsed managing up: "I fully support managing your manager. I think it's something that you have to do if you want an accelerated promotion timeline."
