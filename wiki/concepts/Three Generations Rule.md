## Definition

Tony Fadell's observed pattern that new hardware categories typically take three generations to succeed: "make the product, fix the product, then fix the business." He states he has "never seen anyone get it all right the first time."

## Key Information

- **iPod**: the first two generations sold only to a small base of Mac loyalists (under 1% of the market) and lost momentum after an initial sales bump each quarter; only the third generation — with Windows connectivity and the iTunes Music Store — broke out.
- **iPhone**: the first version only worked on AT&T's slower "2.5G" network and only in the US; it took multiple versions to reach broader viability, alongside internal margin/reliability improvements.
- **Nest**: the smoke detector took two generations, and the thermostat several generations, before the *business* (not just the product) worked.
- The pattern: gen 1 makes the product (often unprofitable — "First iPhones we weren't making any money"), gen 2 refines features and starts improving margins, gen 3 gets the business fundamentals right (margins, volume, reliability, market fit).
- Fadell frames sticking with an idea through failure as necessary unless something is fundamentally, severely flawed: "you got to fail a few times till you find your way... you only fail if you stop. If you keep iterating and keep going, well, then that's not failure. That's called learning" (a view he shares with Jeff Bezos).
- Related tactic: "skunk works" side projects (e.g., iPod Windows connectivity, iPhone stylus) are sometimes pursued against the opinion-based leader's stated wishes, in anticipation of a later generation where they'll be needed.
