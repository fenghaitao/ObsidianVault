## Definition

[[Sam Lesson]]'s central framing for etiquette: the goal of good etiquette isn't politeness for its own sake, but the ability to walk into any room — a VC holiday party, a pitch meeting, a first date — and project calm, trustworthy confidence rather than anxious, transactional urgency.

## Key Information

- Origin quote: "Etiquette is a skill for how to show up in a room with a low heart rate. You're at the Kleiner Perkins holiday party. You have all the venture capitalists in the world and all the CEOs. You're at your first company. You're like, 'Oh my god, this is my shot'... It becomes very transactional."
- The failure mode is showing up "like a little Energizer Bunny" — high-intensity, scarcity-driven energy that "scares people off" and "projects totally the wrong vibe," even when the underlying anxiety ("this really is my one shot") is understandable, especially for young or first-time founders.
- Paired directly with an [[Abundance Mindset]]: the antidote to a racing heart rate is genuinely believing "this is not going to be my only opportunity... this is part of the story, this is not the entire story."
- Framed as increasingly important, not less, as software commoditizes: since founders are asking people to trust them with data and business relationships, and technology is "no longer some cute sideshow," understanding how to meet people where they are and build trust becomes a competitive tool, not a distraction from "just focus on your product."
- Nearly every specific etiquette rule in the episode (being early but not overly early, not over-questioning in conversation, tipping generously but not showily, letting others order first at dinner, not making a production of leaving) is explicitly tied back to this same underlying goal: don't let anxiety/scarcity leak into your visible behavior.
- Complementary analogy used elsewhere in the same conversation: small talk functions like "the TCP/IP handshake" or an old dial-up "modem crash" — brief, low-stakes signal-exchange that has to happen before real communication/business can flow, and that itself lowers the room's collective heart rate.
- Lesson notes a limit to the thesis: true, rare technical breakthroughs ("if you bring mana from heaven... invent something that is literally the next Google") can override the need for any of this — but this describes roughly 0.1% of startups, not the median founder his advice targets.
- Also underlies his "signaling of effort" principle: what often matters in a social interaction is less the polished result and more the visible effort to get it right (e.g., an eye-contact struggle from a neurodivergent founder who is clearly trying reads very differently from someone who visibly doesn't care).
