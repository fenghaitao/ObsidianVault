## Definition

Tony Fadell's framing of storytelling as core product-building craft, not a marketing afterthought: the practice of communicating a product's "why," not just its "what," so it becomes meaningful and relatable to customers.

## Key Information

- Core claim: "too many times when we're technology-led, we talk about the what. We don't talk about the why. And the why is storytelling."
- Steve Jobs is the primary exemplar: he rehearsed and refined the story of the iPhone daily for roughly 2.5 years, testing the pitch on trusted people, so his keynote delivery appeared effortless — "when you saw him come on stage, it was just cuz he had done it... 10,000 times."
- Storytelling is described as fundamental to human nature (movies, books, being "taken on a journey"); good teachers/professors use the same mechanism to make technical material meaningful.
- Fadell's own learning path: watching his father sell Levi's — including sometimes advising a customer *not* to buy, building trust and relationship over an immediate sale — as an early lesson that storytelling is about truth and relationship, not manipulation.
- Genuine storytelling vs. manipulation: infomercials use real psychological/emotional techniques (e.g., a "virus of doubt" that dramatizes the customer's pain) that are useful to study, but should be applied honestly rather than through overhyped expectations — "the best marketing just tells the truth" (Steve Jobs' formulation).
- Practical craft advice: telling the same story to others repeatedly (before shipping) to test whether it resonates is how a founder refines and validates a product narrative — not a one-time flash of inspiration.
- Connected to the press-release-first / [[Working Backwards]] discipline: because only three or four key features survive into a press release before it becomes unintelligible to a customer, storytelling forces early, hard prioritization of what actually matters.
