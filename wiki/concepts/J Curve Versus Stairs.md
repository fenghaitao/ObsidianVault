## Definition

[[Molly Graham]]'s career-growth framework, drawn originally on a whiteboard for her by [[Chamath Palihapitiya]]: most people's careers are a predictable set of "stairs" (promoted every ~2 years, title changes incrementally), but the far more valuable path is a "J-curve" — jumping into a role you're clearly underqualified for, falling (feeling incompetent) for roughly 6-9 months, then climbing out to a level the stairs could never have reached.

## Key Information

- Origin story: while running Facebook's employer-brand/HR work and planning to stay there "till we went public," Graham was recruited by Chamath Palihapitiya (then running growth and mobile) to help build a Facebook-branded mobile phone despite having no relevant background ("you're useless in HR"). Her boss [[Sheryl Sandberg]] told her the project "will be dead in two months, but you can do it because you'll still have a job here."
- The fall: she spent six months "feeling like an absolute idiot," asking what she calls "the dumbest questions of my life" in rooms full of brilliant people, and received the lowest performance rating of her career at the end of that period.
- The climb: roughly three years later she'd become a genuine mobile-hardware expert — she remembers the specific moment she realized it, drawing a phone's internal layout on a whiteboard to explain to Chamath why something he wanted wasn't feasible. The phone project itself was ultimately "a giant, costly failure for Facebook," but not a failure for her personally — it set her up for skills she "never could have dreamed of" and she ended her Facebook tenure in product, business development, and hardware.
- Chamath's original whiteboard drawing: "stairs" careers are boring — walk up, get promoted every two years, title changes from manager → senior manager → director. "J-curve" careers mean jumping off a cliff, falling for about 6-9 months, then climbing to a place "way beyond where the stairs could ever get you."
- Fear-diagnostic distinction Graham draws for deciding whether to jump: **financial fear** is a type of fear worth doing the math on (know your "number" — the monthly amount you need to not be existentially anxious — and decide if you can hit it via savings or consulting work); but fear that's really just "I'm scared I'm not capable of this" is, in her words, "a flashing green light" — the kind of fear that means you should jump and find out.
- "Professional idiot" as the transferable skill of the falling phase: the single most valuable move during the 6-9 month fall is to "embrace being a professional idiot" — showing up and asking the dumb question ("what does that word mean? what are we even talking about?") because (a) you learn enormously regardless of outcome, and (b) it usually turns out other people had the same question and were too afraid to ask it. Graham says her actual "superpower" is being the person who asks "do we have goals? why are we having this meeting?" — bringing clarity by admitting confusion.
- Attributed by [[Sarah Caldwell]] (of OpenAI) as the single framework from Graham that helped her most in her own career; Graham has also given a TED talk on this specific framework.
- Related theme: the framework only works if you can also let go of your prior scope — see "[[Give Away Your Legos]]."
