## Definition

[[Jason Cohen]]'s contrarian take: most A/B testing, as practiced by most companies, doesn't actually work — both because it can't test the things that matter most (strategy, vision) and because most measured "wins" on smaller details are statistical false positives.

## Key Information

- Can't test what matters: fundamental strategic bets ("is this idea even good?") aren't A/B-testable — Cohen's example: "you don't A/B test whether Uber is a good idea."
- The false-positive trap on smaller tests: teams iteratively test copy/design variants, keep the "winning" version each time, and stack these wins over a year — yet often find conversion rates unchanged a year later. Cohen's explanation: testing tools are frequently not statistically rigorous, and even a genuinely 95%-accurate test will still produce more false positives than true positives when the underlying effect being searched for is rare — which is the normal case for small UI/copy tests.
- Real-world corroboration cited by Lenny: a Shopify CTO described running holdout groups that re-test whether measured effects persist — roughly a third of their "wins" disappeared under that scrutiny, despite Shopify having a large, sophisticated experimentation team.
- Not a blanket dismissal: Cohen concedes A/B testing can work well at very high traffic/scale (where statistical power is adequate) or when practiced with real rigor (holdout groups, re-testing) — his critique targets the common, less rigorous practice at typical company scale, where the effort is often a waste of time relative to working on the [[Growth Stall Diagnostic Framework]]'s bigger-lever questions.
