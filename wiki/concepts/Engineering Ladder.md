## Definition

The Engineering Ladder is the structured career progression framework used by tech companies to define levels of seniority, scope, and compensation for individual contributors. Different companies use different nomenclature (E3-E6 at Meta, L3-L6 at Google, Senior/Staff/Principal at others) but the progression of scope is consistent.

## Key Information

- Zach's career progression through the ladder: IC3 (Facebook) → IC4 (Facebook, 5 months) → IC5/Senior (Netflix, via job hop) → IC6/Staff (Airbnb, via job hop)
- At Meta/Facebook, the ladder includes: IC3 (entry level), IC4 (mid-level), IC5 (senior), IC6 (staff)
- At Netflix (at the time), there was no formal ladder — everyone was just "Senior Engineer" with no Staff level; Netflix has since added levels
- Zach was on the Data Engineering (DE) track at Facebook, which is separate from the Software Engineering (SWE) track — DEs get 30-40% less equity than SWEs at the same level
- Zach was told he'd be down-leveled if he switched from DE to SWE at Facebook, which was "standard practice" — going from SWE to DE didn't trigger a down-level, revealing a bias in the ladder
- The transition from senior to staff is described as a shift from "delivering on the work you're supposed to deliver on" to "deciding which problem to solve" and "selling a technical vision"
- Zach's rapid climb (IC3 to IC6 in roughly 3-4 years of actual work) demonstrates that the ladder timeline can be compressed through job hopping
- Compensation grows significantly at each rung: Zach's compensation doubled from Facebook IC4 ($~180K) to Netflix Senior ($380K-$390K), and increased further at Airbnb Staff ($500K+)
- At the UCLA talk, the levels were explained by scope: junior (tasks with help), mid-level (independent tasks), senior (given an area, creates tasks), staff (finds the areas). Junior to mid-level takes 2-4 years; mid-level to senior takes 2-4 years. Senior is the terminal level — most people stop there. Staff is optional and involves setting team strategy and leading others. The "up or out" expectation applies to junior through senior, but not beyond.

### Brian Attwell's Redesigned Ladder
- Attwell critiques Uber/Meta/Amazon ladders for position-oriented competencies ("design processes for the whole company," "partner with directors on strategy," "influence other teams") that reward being "loud and well positioned" and encourage empire building
- His redesign: ~1,000 examples of high performance → 250 human attributes → principal component analysis → 12 skill gradients; compensation rises as you climb the skills that matter
- The main difference is emphasizing "how you perform" (truth seeking) over "what you perform" (scope): e.g., "you have to be right most of the time when questioning a foundational engineering and product decision or industry norm"
- Communication is a separate competency defined with examples, so engineers self-score against concrete artifacts
- Managers can still posture, but now around beneficial skills; the remaining defense is spot-checking managers and firing exaggerators
