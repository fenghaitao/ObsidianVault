## Definition

Impostor Syndrome is the persistent feeling of self-doubt and fear of being exposed as a fraud despite evidence of competence and success. In software engineering, it commonly manifests as anxiety about technical skills, performance, or whether one truly deserves their role.

## Key Information

### Ricky's Experience
- Early in his career, Ricky thought he might be a "personality hire" — someone kept around for social reasons rather than technical ability
- He felt he didn't "hit a home run" during his Google interviews or internship, which contributed to his self-doubt
- At lower levels, he struggled to gauge his own performance and would constantly ask his manager "am I doing okay?", unable to accept reassurance even when told he was doing fine
- He questioned whether his performance was "just okay" like a C grade or if he was doing "an A+"

### Overcoming Strategy: Ask Questions Despite Fear
- Ricky's primary strategy for overcoming impostor syndrome was adopting the mindset: "I'd rather ask questions and look dumb and learn something — and maybe I'll get fired because people think I'm dumb — than to do nothing, not learn anything, not ask any questions, and get fired"
- He recognized that most people are willing to help and want junior colleagues to succeed
- This approach helped him learn unblocking skills and move projects forward independently

### Advice to Younger Self
- Ricky's advice to his younger self: trust your judgment more and have more "audacity" to push boundaries and try new things
- He felt he waited too long to act on many ideas because he was afraid of "rocking the boat," but when he finally did, those projects were very successful
- He believes he could have done more if he had been willing to trust himself earlier

### UCLA Talk: Universality of Impostor Syndrome
- At the UCLA talk, both Ryan and Ricky called imposter syndrome their "best friend" throughout their careers
- Ricky described it as "very normal" and affecting "a majority of people." Even after multiple promotions, he thought "maybe they promoted me wrong" or "maybe they screwed up this promotion." His manager eventually told him "You need to chill" because his anxiety was disproportionate to his performance
- Ryan echoed he was "just an anxious little kid" who was "always stressed out about work" despite getting good marks
- Ricky's manager had to reassure him in a one-on-one: "You need to chill, you know, like you're doing okay, calm down"
- The "delusional" mindset that helped: "I deserve to be here. I have to ask questions." And: "I'm just going to try to learn as much as I can here and if I get fired, I get fired, but at least I learned"

### Philip Su's Experience
- Philip deliberately sought and received a demotion from E9 to E7 when switching from Site Director to IC at Oculus
- He said: "it feels so much better to be performing well at a level than it feels to be barely treading water at a level that you feel a lot of impostor syndrome in"
- He was "very much welcomed being leveled appropriately relative to my contributions" rather than being over-leveled and feeling fraudulent
- He also had to be careful not to use his "outsize influence from the past to get things done" — he needed to support the new leaders by playing his role on the team

### Carey Nachenberg's Experience
- At Symantec, Carey felt safe because he was well-regarded there, but feared he "grew up" there and wouldn't be good enough elsewhere (Google, Meta, etc.) — which kept him comfortable but unhappy near the end
- The thing that broke the inertia was an external pull: someone reached out to interview him, and he decided "I'm probably going to fail this interview... but I'm going to do it"
- Impostor syndrome returned at Google: at senior levels he was afraid to risk a big project not landing ("what have you been doing the last couple months?") because he lacked confidence the leadership would have his back, unlike at Symantec
- At X he saw a 200+ IQ engineer stuck at L4 due to poor communication and low business impact — ability alone is not what gets recognized

### Dwayne Reeves' Experience
- Early in his career Dwayne wrestled with "a lot of impostor syndrome," rooted in measuring his worth only by his output — the code he could type
- The clarity that helped: "I'm more than what I can type on the keyboard" — his value is his ideas and the decisions about what to build, not being an output machine

### David Fowler's Experience
- Reaching Distinguished Engineer (the same level as Guido van Rossum) gave him "maximum imposter syndrome"; he called himself a "baby DE" for the first year
- His early reframe: promotions are about making an impact, not being the smartest — "being smart is something else"
- Mentors redirected him from fixing every weakness toward amplifying strengths, reducing the pressure to be perfectly well-rounded

### Boris Cherny's Experience
- Boris felt impostor syndrome directing engineers at his own (hidden) level on the public-groups project
- His reframe: "don't overthink it — no one really knows what they're doing"; levels don't matter that much, and a little impostor syndrome is healthy because it signals you're pushing yourself ([[summary-20251215 - Boris Cherny (Creator of Claude Code) On What Grew His Career And Building at Anthropic]])
