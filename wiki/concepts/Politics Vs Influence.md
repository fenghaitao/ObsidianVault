## Definition

[[Jessica Fain]]'s distinction between two things people often conflate: "politics" is manipulating outcomes and people for personal gain, while "influence" is increasing the odds that genuinely good ideas survive and get funded — a skill built on curiosity and empathy, not manipulation.

## Key Information

- Addresses a common objection ("this feels icky/manipulative, I just want to do great work") by reframing stakeholder management as a discovery/learning exercise rather than a persuasion campaign — treat executive conversations like user-research interviews, aimed at strengthening your own idea, not just extracting approval.
- Fain's litmus test: if you don't respect a stakeholder or don't believe they know something you don't, that's a signal to leave the organization, not a license to bypass them — but if you do respect them, engaging with their feedback genuinely (not as a rubber-stamp formality) produces both better relationships and better products.
- Illustrated by the story of Noah Weiss (Slack CPO) keeping an unprompted personal notebook of things he learned from Stewart Butterfield over years — done purely for genuine learning, which later became source material for codifying Slack's product principles — contrasted with "politicking" behavior done for appearances.
- Practical implication: going into a meeting purely "looking for approval" is a failure mode; going in to learn, using the executive's domain expertise to strengthen the plan, produces a better outcome and a better relationship simultaneously.
