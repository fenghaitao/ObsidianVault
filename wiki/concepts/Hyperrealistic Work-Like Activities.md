## Definition

[[Stewart Butterfield]]'s term for meetings, decks, and analysis that are structurally indistinguishable from genuine work — people in a room, something projected on a screen, discussion happening — but produce no meaningful value, arising when an organization has more people (and more "[[Parkinson's Law (Organizational)|Parkinson's Law]]"-driven headcount) than it has "known valuable work to do."

## Key Information

- Companion concept: "known valuable work to do" — work where you both know *what* it is and know *that* it's valuable. Early-stage startups are full of this (open a bank account, build a users table, salt passwords); as companies scale, the supply of known-valuable work fails to keep pace with headcount growth, and people fill the gap with hyperrealistic work-like activities without realizing that's what they're doing.
- Concrete real example from Slack: at launch, thread replies auto-populated the input box with "@[previous poster]" — Butterfield removed it, arguing it taught the wrong mental model (every prior poster in a thread already gets notified regardless). About 6 months later it was quietly reinstated, based on an analysis (feature-flagged A/B test, dashboards, meetings, presentation decks) showing threads were marginally longer with it (2.17 vs. 2.14 messages on average) — a difference Butterfield calls not even clearly statistically significant, and in any case far smaller than the actual organizational cost (engineering, QA, analytics, product, multiple meetings) of running the analysis that "proved" it. He calls this a "guaranteed loser" regardless of the analysis's direction, since the maximum conceivable value of the change was dwarfed by the cost of deciding it.
- Not attributable to individual malice or stupidity — even senior people (board members, executives) fall into it, and it becomes easier to fall into the further someone is from full context, information, and real decision-making authority.
- **Fix, per Butterfield**: it's a leadership responsibility (manager, director, executive, CEO) to actively ensure there is sufficient supply of known valuable work and communicate priorities/say no to unnecessary initiatives clearly — not to blame individual contributors for filling time with work-like activities, since that framing implies they're being lazy or dumb when the actual failure is upstream clarity and prioritization.
