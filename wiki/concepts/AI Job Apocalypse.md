## Definition

The narrative — pushed back on by [[Dan Shipper]] — that AI will directly and broadly eliminate large swaths of jobs, which he argues is overblown alongside the related "[[SaaS Apocalypse]]" narrative.

## Key Information

- Shipper's rebuttal rests on [[Automation Is A Lie]]: every AI agent still needs a human maintaining and directing it, which is durable, ongoing work (see [[Forward Deployed Engineer]]), not work that simply disappears.
- Benchmarks like [[METR]]'s measurement of how long a model can work autonomously (e.g., ~17 hours at 50% accuracy for the model Shipper calls "Mythos") can make an AI job apocalypse look inevitable by showing raw capability growth — but Shipper argues capability growth doesn't translate directly into full job elimination.
- Connects to his "[[Allocation Economy]]" essay: human work with AI increasingly resembles management (overseeing, correcting, directing AI "reports") rather than being replaced outright.
- He is "super bullish" specifically on product managers and full-stack designers as roles that expand rather than shrink under AI, consistent with his broader argument.
- Parallels [[Benedict Evans]]'s independent argument (episode 07) via [[Task Vs Job]] and [[Jevons Paradox]] that automating individual tasks doesn't straightforwardly eliminate the jobs those tasks sit inside.
- **Contrasting anecdote from [[Lazar]] (episode 27)**: offers a more pessimistic "horses" analogy (attributed to a person he says works at Anthropic on Claude Code, via a tweet) — comparing AI-driven job displacement to how steam engines took ~200 years to develop but eradicated ~90% of the US horse population within 20 years once cars arrived; Lazar's point is that AI-driven professional displacement is happening even faster than that, citing a technical-writer role that reportedly became obsolete within six months. This is a different guest's anecdote/prediction, not a factual claim about the same specific case Dan Shipper discusses — presented here as a contrasting perspective on displacement speed, not a resolved contradiction.
