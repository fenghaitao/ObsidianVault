## Definition

Bryan Cantrill is a software engineer and co-founder/CTO of Oxide Computer. He was a Distinguished Engineer at Sun Microsystems and later CTO of Joyent. (Transcription note: the transcript body renders his first name as "Brian" in places; the correct spelling is "Bryan," matching the episode title.)

## Key Information

- Graduated in 1996 and joined Sun Microsystems' Solaris kernel development team, drawn by meeting energized engineers Kevin Clark and Jeff Bonwick.
- Refused to consider Microsoft "as a point of principle," believing Bill Gates-era Microsoft's products robbed him of his adolescence with unprotected-memory DOS/Windows software, and he saw Microsoft as a compiler company in its DNA, not an operating-systems company.
- Held Sun rank-like titles as Member of Technical Staff and rose to Distinguished Engineer; he emphasizes he was never focused on grade or promotion and preferred being under-promoted to over-promoted.
- Views formalized performance review as measurement rather than an instrument for improving performance; calls stack ranking "organizational cancer" and says teams do extraordinary things, not adverarial ranking.
- Describes his Distinguished Engineer promotion as validated by Sun CTO Greg Papadopoulos, who presented his case to the committee and told the DEs he expected no votes against it.
- After Sun was acquired by Oracle, he left within roughly 45 days, feeling ashamed to work for a company that did not have its customers' trust; he later moved to Joyent, where he competed with AWS, served as VP of Engineering and then CTO, and built the Triton (and El Ax) compute lines.
- Calls Jeff Bezos "an apex predator in capitalism" for Amazon's relentless implementation and price cuts that made the cloud smart-bid look terrible while preserving margins.
- Started Oxide Computer in 2019 with Steve Tuck after realizing the venture he wanted to build was a rack-scale computer; he describes Oxide's team as organized like a heist movie, with each person's skills arriving at the right moment.
- Regrets a 1997(ish) Usenet reply, an SNL-born quip ("have you ever kissed a girl?"), as a "regret column" moment; otherwise says his mistakes — including his self-described worst hire who turned out to be hired after Joyent — became load-bearing lessons.
- Refuses to view Sun as a "failure": it was a Fortune 200 public company for decades despite not surviving to the scope of its own ambition.
