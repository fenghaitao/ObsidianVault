## Definition

Ryan Williams is an MIT professor and complexity theorist who won the Gödel Prize; his 2025 breakthrough showed that any computation running in time t can be simulated using only √t space.

## Key Information

- Improved on the t/log t space bound that had stood for about 50 years (previously the best known for saving space at the cost of much more time).
- His algorithm uses an earlier result by James Cook and Ian Mertz as an essential technical ingredient.
- The simulated algorithm runs in a lot of time, but the result shows time and space are related in a highly non-trivial way.
- Introduced in this episode as "a professor at MIT who won the Gödel Prize for theoretical computer science"; the conversation opened with the three-sum problem.
- His breakthrough intuition: reading James Cook and Ian Mertz's tree-evaluation paper carefully, plus the XOR-swap trick (swapping two registers via XOR without a temp) — writing only into erased/blank memory is unnecessarily restrictive.
- On fine-grained complexity: he studies whether canonical textbook time bounds (n², n³, 2ⁿ) are optimal, and relates problems through reductions that preserve small running-time improvements.
- Is on the record as NOT believing the Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis (SETH), though he finds assuming it false operationally productive: "hypotheses at the edge of our understanding can be enlightening."
- Assigned contrarian confidences in a paper on the likelihood of complexity conjectures: 80% P ≠ NP, 45% EXP ≠ NEXP, 80% NEXP = coNEXP — arguing we don't understand polynomial-time computation as deeply as we think.
- Career advice: you don't need permission to work on hard problems, and you should avoid inertia through periodic self-evaluation (comparing your past and present self).
