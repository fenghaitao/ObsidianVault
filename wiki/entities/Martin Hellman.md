## Definition

Martin Hellman is a computer scientist who, with Whitfield Diffie and Ralph Merkle, co-invented public key cryptography, for which he won the Turing Award.

## Key Information

- Born October 1945 in the Bronx; raised Jewish in an Irish Catholic neighborhood, which he credits for his "street fighter" willingness to take on unpopular, "crazy" work.
- Earned a PhD in information theory at Stanford under Tom Cover, worked at IBM Research (Yorktown Heights) in 1968, taught at MIT from 1969–71, and returned to the Stanford faculty in 1971.
- Began cryptography on his own time, unaware of any export restriction; the NSA later argued his international-journal publications violated ITAR (cryptographic material classified as an implement of war).
- With Whitfield Diffie (from fall 1974) developed public key cryptography; completed Diffie-Hellman key exchange in May 1976 and published it in the IEEE Transactions on Information Theory (November 1976 issue, actually released February 1977).
- Won the Turing Award, the top prize in computer science, for public key cryptography.
- Later work ("rethinking national security") argues a child born today has worse-than-even odds of living a natural life because of nuclear weapons alone; he advocates HR 3564 to restrict the president's sole nuclear-launch authority.
- Personal: married 59 years to Dorothy; influenced by Creative Initiative, founded by Stanford professor Harry Rathbun; describes himself as "a follower of Jesus" while remaining Jewish.
- Normalization: episode speech-to-text garbles "Whit" Diffie as "Witt"; "Louis Ignarro" (1998 Nobel in Physiology or Medicine) as "Lou and Yaro"; the Swedish town Ronneby as "Runneby"; and Stanford's Stephen Pohlig (of Pohlig–Hellman) as just "Steve."
