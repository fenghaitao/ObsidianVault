## Definition

Global pharmaceutical company (one of the largest in the world), descended from the Nordisk Insulin Laboratorium founded in 1920s Denmark by [[Marie Krogh]], [[August Krogh]], and Canadian insulin researchers. Central historical case study in [[Eric Ries]]'s *Incorruptible* for an "industrial foundation" governance structure that has protected the company's ethos for over 100 years.

## Key Information

- Origin story: August Krogh, a Nobel laureate physiologist, and his wife Marie Krogh (one of Denmark's first credentialed female doctors, later diagnosed with then-fatal diabetes) learned of a new Canadian technology to isolate insulin during a 1920s North American lecture tour. They negotiated to commercialize it, worried from the outset about the temptation to "harvest what others plant" — i.e., to extract monopoly profits from desperate, dependent patients.
- Incorporated the resulting company (Nordisk Insulin Laboratorium, predecessor to Novo Nordisk) as a for-profit company owned and governed by a nonprofit foundation — a two-tiered structure called an **industrial foundation**, decades before this kind of concern became a mainstream business topic (Ries contrasts it with the later Martin Shkreli price-gouging scandal).
- This structure has endured for more than a hundred years and reportedly once required the nonprofit foundation's trustees to intervene directly to block a temptation to sell out the for-profit company — an intervention Ries says ultimately created more than $500 billion in shareholder value.
- Academic research cited by Ries: companies with this "industrial foundation" structure (like Novo Nordisk and Zeiss) are six times more likely to survive to year 50 than conventional counterparts, and show superior return on invested capital.
- Cited as one of the two simplest "spiritual holding company" templates (nonprofit foundation ownership), alongside the perpetual purpose trust used by Patagonia.
