## Definition

Barbara Liskov is a Turing Award-winning computer scientist at MIT known for inventing data abstraction, the CLU programming language, and the Liskov substitution principle, and for distributed-systems work including Argus, Viewstamped Replication, and Byzantine fault tolerance.

## Key Information

- Rejected by Princeton's graduate program with a postcard reading "We do not admit women"; did her undergraduate math degree at Berkeley and entered computer science via a programming job after deciding against a math PhD.
- Invented her notion of modularity at Mitre, wrote the data-abstraction paper with Steve Zilles, and realized it in CLU; it reached industry via Ada and Java.
- Developed Argus, a CLU-influenced object-oriented distributed language whose "guardians" communicated by remote procedure call and whose computations ran as atomic transactions borrowed from databases.
- Co-developed Viewstamped Replication (with Brian Oki) and Byzantine fault tolerance (with Miguel Castro) — the latter prompted by a DARPA request for proposals about Byzantine attacks.
- Namesake of the Liskov substitution principle, from a 1986 OOPSLA keynote and formalized with Jeannette Wing as behavioral subtyping.
- Advises students to avoid incremental work: find a good problem that is amenable to solution and matches your skill set, and recognize when a direction is bad.
- On the "why did she get the Turing Award?" backlash, she reads it as a compliment — her field's foundations became so basic people forgot there was a "before."
- Served as James Cowling's PhD advisor at MIT; he worked with her on abstraction and the value of clean designs that minimize complexity.
