## Definition

Google is a technology company whose products (Gmail, Google Calendar, clock app) are used by Butterfield as recurring examples of poor UX decisions that persist despite massive scale.

## Key Information

- Google Calendar (iOS): time zone picker presents all time zones in alphabetical order, making it nearly unusable for frequent travelers. Butterfield calls this "bananas how bad it is" for an app used by hundreds of millions
- Gmail: actions on individual emails are split into two long menus that are inconsistent and confusing; dragging recipients between To/CC fields requires very high dexterity
- Gmail app: Patrick Collison (CEO of Stripe) directly complained to Sundar Pichai about the recipient dragging UX
- iPhone clock app: the "Sleep" feature is described as incomprehensible (ungrammatical, unclear what it does, what it costs)
- Butterfield notes that despite these issues, no one switches platforms because of a bad time zone picker — but there is real value in delighting customers and forming emotional connections
- Gemini 3 launch reportedly caused OpenAI a "code red" moment, with ~6% market share loss in a week, demonstrating competitive volatility in AI

- Molly Graham was an early employee at Google, which she describes as feeling like a "university" and "two PhD students paradise" — where ideas were more important than what shipped and the campus was designed for people to live there
- Contrasted with Facebook's "19-year-old hacker's dorm room" culture: Google valued ideas, Facebook valued shipping
- Co-work (Claude) can integrate with Google Docs and Gmail for automated tasks
- [[Qasar Younis]] worked at Google in the late 2000s/early 2010s when Google was the "apex predator of Silicon Valley" — the #1 company everyone wanted to work at
- Qasar witnessed Google's failure to compete with Facebook: Google had the best engineers, $1B/month cash flow, and 15-20x Facebook's headcount, but couldn't win social because "a gorilla can't learn to fly by not being a gorilla"
- This illustrates how large companies fail when momentum in one direction overwhelms market-change signals
- Nikhyl Singhal was a long-time executive at Google, where he observed that "probably you need like 500 people to keep the lights on" out of 25-40,000 — a 90% overhead ratio
- Nikhyl notes that Google birthed the APM program because traditional product management (from HP, Cisco, AMD era) didn't fit internet companies — they created a new breed of PM
