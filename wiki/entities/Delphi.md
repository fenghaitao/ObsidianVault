## Definition

Delphi (transcribed phonetically as "Deli" throughout the source transcript) is a platform for building AI "digital clones" of a person or brand, trained on that person's full body of content. [[Lenny Rachitsky]] used it to build "Lennybot"; [[Jason Lemkin]] later used it to build SaaStr's general-purpose GTM agent ("Digital Jason"), which became the seed of SaaStr's entire AI go-to-market transition.

## Key Information

- Lenny's usage ("Lennybot," at lennybot.com): ingests every interview Lenny has done and everything he's written, letting users ask synthesized questions across all of it (e.g., combining lessons from separate guest episodes); has a voice mode that reproduces Lenny's voice.
- [[Brian Halligan]] (founder/chairman of [[HubSpot]]) built his own version using Delphi, before Lemkin did; Lemkin says what he found most compelling about Halligan's was that it channeled the collective voices of Halligan's guests, not just Halligan himself — the same design Lenny used.
- Lemkin's usage: built a combined "Digital Jason"/general SaaStr support-and-sales agent after being inspired by both Lenny's and Halligan's clones; ingesting his ~10,000 pieces of content initially broke the product (too much data), taking about a week to get working.
- Origin of SaaStr's AI GTM pivot: this general-purpose agent, not trained specifically for sales or GTM, autonomously closed a $70,000 sponsorship deal ahead of SaaStr Annual — the proof point that convinced Lemkin to aggressively deploy specialized agents (see [[Artisan]], [[Qualified]]) across the rest of the sales funnel.
- Secondary use case: users unexpectedly began asking the SaaStr clone event-logistics questions (refunds, venue directions, speaker lineups); it began answering these on its own after Lemkin's team, previously using Intercom and answering support tickets up to two weeks late, could not keep up — illustrating Lemkin's broader advice that support is often the best place to start with agentic AI.
- Early failure mode: initially gave users wrong event dates, requiring Lemkin to spend about an hour each morning reviewing and correcting its answers — a burden that has since disappeared as the agent became well-trained (see [[Agent Training Loop]]).
