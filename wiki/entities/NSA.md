## Definition

The National Security Agency (NSA) is the U.S. signals-intelligence agency that, in the 1970s, opposed the open publication of cryptographic research and argued it violated export laws.

## Key Information

- Maintained that Martin Hellman and Whitfield Diffie were breaking the law by publishing cryptographic papers in international journals, citing ITAR (International Traffic in Arms Regulations), which classified anything cryptographic as an implement of war.
- In July 1977, an NSA-affiliated individual wrote from his Maryland home address to the IEEE, claiming IEEE members were breaking the law by publishing certain papers; NSA operatives at conferences wore name badges reading "Department of Defense" as a "simple substitution cipher" for NSA.
- Tried to recruit Hellman as a consultant early on, offering classified work; Hellman declined because they would not permit him to publish independently.
- Hellman estimated NSA had a multi-billion-dollar budget and a decades head start; NSA also had enough problems and funds to keep an exhaustive key-search machine fully loaded — a "crude form of trapdoor" ordinary users lacked.
- Two NSA people moved over to NBS (National Bureau of Standards) and influenced the short 56-bit DES key size.
- Counterparts mentioned: GCHQ (British; claims to have invented public key cryptography slightly earlier in secret, but with nothing on digital signatures) and GRU (Soviet/Russian).
