## Definition
Arm is the family of reduced-instruction-set (RISC) processor designs — originating at the English company Acorn Computers — that now dominate mobile devices and are spreading into personal computers and the cloud.
## Key Information
- Origin: Acorn Computers (England) wanted its own instruction set and chip for a personal computer; unsatisfied with available chips and influenced by "the papers we did at Berkeley," it built the "Acorn RISC Machine."
- Apple, seeking a microprocessor for its Newton device (an early forerunner of the iPhone), asked Acorn to drop the name; the "Acorn RISC Machine" became the "Advanced RISC Machine" (ARM), which Apple used in the Newton.
- The Newton was not a commercial success, but it demonstrated RISC's benefits for mobile devices; Nokia embraced ARM in one of its first popular cellular phones.
- Scale: ~350 billion ARM-based processors have shipped; Patterson estimates ~99% of all processors today are RISC.
- Apple subsequently switched personal computers from x86 to ARM, and Amazon, Microsoft, and Google are each developing their own ARM processors for the cloud — Patterson frames the x86 market as shrinking while the RISC market "grows leaps and bounds."
- Transcription notes: the transcript renders "RISC Machine" as "risk/wrist machine" and the Nokia phone network as "GDM" (likely GSM); normalized here.
