## Definition

Sun Microsystems was the workstation and server company (founded 1983) where Bryan Cantrill spent 14 years working on the Solaris kernel and rose to Distinguished Engineer, before its acquisition by Oracle in 2009.

## Key Information

- In 1996 Sun was, in Cantrill's view, the only company still truly invested in its own operating system (Solaris), during what he calls Unix's darkest hour.
- Sun rewarded technical boldness and gave its engineers tremendous freedom, attracting people who wanted to solve hard, commercially relevant problems — a contrast with Microsoft's "mortgaging its future to Windows."
- Its engineering career ladder included Member of Technical Staff (MTS) levels, then Staff Engineer, Senior Staff Engineer, and Distinguished Engineer; Cantrill suspects this ladder may originate at Sun or Xerox PARC.
- Sun's performance grades were drawn from "superlative, excellent, good" distributions; a superlative forced a promotion, which made managers hand out apologetic "excellent" grades to avoid promoting too fast.
- Sun's KEEP (Key Employee Incentive Program) was an annual dividend-style bonus pool tied to company performance; it went to zero after the dot-com crash.
- The Distinguished Engineer process had two doors: a political vote by existing DEs (a "country club") and popping in the side via acqui-hire (a company's CTO demanding a DE title during acquisition).
- Sun resisted layoffs under Scott McNealy before eventually running ~35 rounds over eight years; engineers tracked departures daily via an "obits" script against the employee directory ("Sun's strength and weakness was that no one was truly in charge").
- Sun lost 98% of its value in the public markets and traded below cash at one point; IBM and HP jockeyed to buy it punitively before Oracle swept in.
- Cantrill does not view Sun as a failure — a Fortune 200 company for decades whose epitaph, per Scott McNealy, was "Kick butt, had fun, didn't she? Loved her customers, changed computing forever."
