## Definition

Scheduling-link tool ("Calendarly" in the transcript's phonetic rendering) that [[Sam Lesson]] singles out as a running example of poor etiquette — offloading scheduling effort onto the other party rather than genuinely making things easy for them.

## Key Information

- Lesson says he is "fairly famous for hating Calendarly" and once went on "this diet tribe" (rant) about how disrespectful it is, which he says drove enough engagement that he got messages from the company's board — "I got like millions of engagements that meaningfully drove their month... no bad press, no bad tweets."
- His core objection: the default in scheduling shouldn't be "here's my link, you figure it out" — it should be that the less senior/less busy person defers to the more senior/busier person's actual availability, then makes their own schedule work around it. Sending a real link with genuine open options is fine; sending a rigid, sparse link is not.
- Notes a common Calendly failure mode: someone can guess or discover a person's Calendarly URL and self-book a meeting the owner never agreed to (Lesson cites this happening to him with an uninvited founder pitch).
- Acknowledges it's fine to offer a scheduling link *as a fallback* ("do you have a Calendarly, or if not, here's mine in case it's easier") as long as the requester is doing real work to make it easy for the other person, not offloading effort onto them.
- Lesson personally avoids using scheduling-link tools altogether and instead manually adjusts his own calendar based on who's asking and how important the meeting is — an approach he admits is "against the...flat hierarchy" ideal and can read as disrespectful of some people's time, but which he sees as realistic.
