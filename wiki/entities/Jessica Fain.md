## Definition

Product leader with roles at [[Box]], [[Slack]], [[Brightwheel]], and now [[Webflow]]; became a chief-of-staff to [[April Underwood]] and later [[Tamar Yehoshua]] at Slack after directly asking for the role, an experience that shaped her expertise in executive influence — the subject of this episode.

## Key Information

- Central thesis: most people misunderstand how executives actually make decisions, because they center their own desires/motivations instead of empathizing with the executive's context — the same curiosity and empathy PMs apply to users should be applied to executives and stakeholders. See [[Strobe Light Calendar Model]].
- Distinguishes "politics" (manipulating outcomes and people for personal gain) from "influence" (increasing the odds that good ideas survive) — argues people conflate the two and then feel icky about a skill that's actually just structured empathy. See [[Politics Vs Influence]].
- Core tactical advice: spend the first 30-60 seconds of any exec meeting re-establishing context (why we're here, what happened last time, today's goal) since executives context-switch constantly and haven't been "centering" your problem between meetings.
- On presenting to executives: don't lead with exhaustive proof-of-work (put it in an appendix instead); do show that multiple options were considered (a single option reads as "you missed something"), citing Barbara Minto's "Minto Pyramid" (lead with the recommendation, then the options explored, then the evidence) as a related model. See [[Minto Pyramid Principle]].
- Recommends asking "that's so interesting — what led you to believe that?" rather than treating disagreement as an obstacle to argue past — a way to co-create with, rather than fight, a stakeholder's stated opinion.
- Recommends asking people close to an executive (their EA, chief of staff, colleagues who've pitched them successfully) what's actually top-of-mind, rather than asking the executive that generic question directly, which she says has become a hollow, over-used phrase.
- Advocates tightly aligning any pitch to the executive's actual incentives/success metrics (their OKRs, board pressure, what they're measured on) — and, symmetrically, using that same incentive alignment to guide ongoing roadmap work, not just one-off pitches.
- Describes "[[Customer Love Sprint]]" (a two-week, engineer-choice bug-bash/craft sprint at Slack) as an example of aligning team work with an executive-perceived cultural gap (loss of product "craft"/polish).
- Describes "Stuart plus two more" (a Slack design-review norm: build what Stewart Butterfield asked for, plus two alternative versions, to create room for debate) as a technique for balancing "have a point of view" with "stay open to feedback." See [[Stuart Plus Two More]].
- On building trust: don't pitch ideas that contradict an executive's strongly-held beliefs; instead build trust through delivered results, and use "shrinking the change" (from the book *[[Switch]]*) — proposing small, low-risk experiments/proofs-of-concept rather than asking for large upfront commitments. See [[Shrinking The Change]].
- States that proactively killing/deprioritizing your own ideas is one of the highest-trust-building moves available to a product leader, since it signals alignment with company-level incentives over personal ones.
- On AI: argues AI shifts PM leverage away from being the "Gantt-chart master"/best synthesizer of data and toward deciding what survives and building buy-in for it — calls this "a golden age of product management" (the discipline) even as the traditional PM-as-executor role changes. Frames the same "onboard with curiosity, empathize with constraints" playbook as directly applicable to directing AI agents themselves, since a team is now increasingly composed of them.
- Views distribution as an underrated emerging bottleneck (cites Gemini's distribution-driven traction over product superiority as an example) and trust-building (with both users and internal teams) as remaining durably valuable regardless of how good AI gets.
