## Definition

Apple smartphone co-created by Tony Fadell; central case study for opinion-based decision-making (touchscreen keyboard vs. physical keyboard, stylus) and for how new technology convergence enables innovation. Also cited by [[Mark Pincus]] as the textbook example of his "[[Proven Better New]]" framework.

## Key Information

- The keyboard decision (on-screen multi-touch vs. physical, BlackBerry-style keyboard) was "the most heated conversation" in development; data was inconclusive on both sides, so Steve Jobs made the final opinion-based call to go all-in on the virtual keyboard.
- Enabled by a convergence of just-emerging technologies: multi-touch, ubiquitous Wi-Fi, the promise of 3G, digital cameras/video, and existing services like YouTube — none of which BlackBerry (primarily a texting device) had combined.
- Took ~2.5 years to develop and, like the iPod, needed multiple hardware/software generations before becoming a true commercial success (it initially only worked on AT&T's slower "2.5G" network in the US).
- Jobs refused a stylus ("your finger is good enough"); Fadell's team developed one anyway as a "skunk works" project, and it later became a valued professional/creative feature without displacing finger-first interaction.
- Internally debated whether to call it the "iPod phone" or "iPhone" due to Cisco's existing iPhone trademark.
- Fadell argues the "iPhone" was really the iPhone + App Store + iTunes system, not just the device — another example of full-stack, systemic innovation.
- Used as the reference point for Fadell's vision of future devices: near-term, devices stay app/touch-centric because voice isn't yet trusted; long-term, input should flip to voice-primary with a persistent (if foldable) display.
- Per [[Mark Pincus]]: cited as the archetypal example of [[Proven Better New]] — proven (existing phone/iPod design patterns), better (refinement of those patterns), new (the touchscreen). Pincus recounts Steve Jobs being visibly obsessed with an MIT team's early touchscreen demo (on a giant whiteboard) at a TED conference, which Pincus identifies as the origin of the iPhone's "new" element.
