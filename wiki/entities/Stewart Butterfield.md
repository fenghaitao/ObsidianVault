## Definition

Founder of [[Flickr]] and [[Slack]] (sold to Salesforce), a famously reclusive product/founder legend who rarely does podcasts. Previously attempted a large-scale mass-market MMO game (Glitch) before pivoting to the internal communication tool his team built for themselves, which became Slack.

## Key Information

- Repeatedly starts game companies that turn into unexpected, bigger hit companies (per Pincus).
- Cited as an example of being humbled by an over-ambitious original vision (a big MMO) into a small, focused pivot (an internal tool for the team) that became a durable hit — illustrating "being less ambitious" as a path to bigger outcomes.
- Born name Dharma Jeremy Butterfield; changed his name to "Stewart" at age 12, wanting to seem "normal" — a choice he now considers a mistake ("in the collective consciousness, a terrible name").

### Per episode 44 (his own dedicated interview)

- Left Salesforce (which acquired Slack) roughly 2.5 years before this episode; currently mostly focused on family, philanthropy, and personal creative projects, with no new company announced. Long-time collaborator [[Cal Henderson]] (CTO of both Flickr and Slack, a 23-year working relationship) periodically discusses what to build next.
- **[[Utility Curves]]**: his core framework for deciding whether a feature/product dimension has crossed the threshold from "no value" to "real value" — used at Slack to decide whether continued investment in something was worthwhile or already past the point of diminishing returns.
- **[[Divine Discontent]]** (crediting Bezos): the bar for "good enough" keeps rising as adjacent software (login, checkout, search) improves, meaning under-iterated features silently fall behind even without changing.
- **[[Comprehension Vs Friction]]**: his central reframing of product design — most of a product's design challenge is comprehension (does the user understand what this is and what to do next), not click-count/friction reduction, which only matters when user intent is already high and specific. Popularized internally via the mantra "[[Don't Make Me Think|don't make me think]]."
- **The umbrella story**: coined "tilting your umbrella is our opportunity" (a riff on Bezos's "your margin is my opportunity") after observing, with Slack's creative director Brandon Velestuk, that only ~1/3 of pedestrians in the rain would tilt their umbrella to let oncoming walkers pass — used at Slack as a parable for how small acts of consideration/craft compound into real competitive advantage and emotional customer loyalty, driving much of Slack's organic, word-of-mouth growth between companies.
- Concrete Slack product-craft decisions: greenlit the "email + magic link" login flow (to avoid password entry on mobile) — an idea from a team member (possibly Ben Brown), not himself, though Slack was among the first to scale it; deliberately kept default notifications noisy for brand-new accounts (since early testers complained the product "didn't work" without them) before nudging users to saner defaults after ~10 messages; created the "shouty rooster" — a deliberately obnoxious animated warning before an @channel/@everyone broadcast — to curb notification-abuse ("tragedy of the commons" dynamics); designed an elaborate, cascading-override rollout for Do Not Disturb (org default → admin override → individual override → admin re-override) to avoid conflict across millions of differently-configured users.
- **[[Hyperrealistic Work-Like Activities]]** and "known valuable work to do": his theory for why large organizations fill up with meetings/analysis that are structurally identical to real work but produce no value — illustrated by a real Slack example where a multi-week, multi-team analytics effort validated pre-populating an "@" mention in thread replies based on a statistically marginal difference (2.17 vs. 2.14 messages per thread) dwarfed by the cost of measuring it.
- **[[Parkinson's Law (Organizational)]]**: headcount/scope expand to fill available time and resources, driven by rational self-interest (career trajectory, status) rather than malice or stupidity — illustrated with Parkinson's original 1950s Royal Navy chart and his own experience with Slack PMs who immediately wanted a direct report.
- **"[[We Don't Sell Saddles Here]]"**: his famous internal positioning memo, written when Slack was ~8 people and unlaunched, later published verbatim; argues a product must sell the outcome it enables, not its literal feature, and cites the classic *[[Positioning (book)|Positioning]]* insight that it's far easier to build on ideas people already hold than implant an entirely new one.
- **[[Owner's Delusion]]**: crowd-sourced term (credited to a now-deleted Twitter account) for why builders routinely fail their own product's most obvious use cases, because they're too close to it to see it as an outside user would (e.g., restaurant websites hiding their address/phone/hours behind slow-loading Ken Burns-effect photos).
- **Pivoting philosophy**: pivot only once every realistic, non-ridiculous option has genuinely been exhausted (as with his own MMO, Glitch, after several years and ~$9M remaining) — and make the decision coldly rational rather than emotional, since sustaining a failing venture is often easier than admitting defeat to investors, employees, and users. Distinguishes real pivots from early-stage iteration. Cites [[Annie Duke]]'s book *Quit* (which uses the Glitch→Slack story as a case study) on treating this as an expected-value decision.
- **Limitless-improvement mindset**: cites [[Ray Dalio]]'s Bridgewater book recounting Michael Jordan's approach to learning to ski — wanting his instructor to point out exactly what he was doing wrong after every run, treating each correction as a "gem" — as his model for how a leader/organization should regard continuous improvement: if you can't see near-limitless opportunity to improve a product (even a tiny feature), you shouldn't be the one designing or running it. Requires a high-trust organization where blunt feedback isn't taken personally.
- **On institutional difficulty**: cites the book *[[Why Nothing Works]]* — despite disliking its repetitive writing — for its core insight that the progressively increasing number of "vetoes" available to block any course of action (in permitting, construction, organizations generally) explains why so little actually gets done, tying back to his own "[[Parkinson's Law (Organizational)]]" and "[[Hyperrealistic Work-Like Activities]]" frameworks for organizational friction.
- **Generosity as game theory**: frames his own reputation for personal and company-level generosity (helping an employee with cash before a holiday, extending severance and job-search help after layoffs, covering 100% of employee health insurance, a "100x money back" downtime SLA that cost Slack roughly $8M in forward credits after a major post-IPO outage, pandemic-era billing relief for struggling customers) as rational cooperate-first behavior in an iterated prisoner's dilemma, reinforced by a company-wide chanted mantra: "in the long run, the measure of our success will be the amount of value that we create for customers."
- Recounts Patrick Collison (Stripe) using rare direct access to Sundar Pichai (Google CEO) at an industry event to complain about the poor drag-and-drop UX for moving email recipients between To/CC fields in Gmail — cited as an example of genuinely great product people being perpetually, specifically dissatisfied with software details most people would never think to complain about.
