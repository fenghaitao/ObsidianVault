## Core Summary

[[Lenny Rachitsky]] interviews [[Edwin Chen]], founder/CEO of [[Surge AI]] — the AI training-data company that hit $1 billion in revenue in under four years with fewer than 100 people, fully bootstrapped and profitable from day one, powering training at every major frontier lab (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google). Chen argues most people misunderstand what "data quality" means: rather than checking boxes (is this technically a poem, does it mention the moon), Surge trains toward genuinely excellent, surprising, emotionally resonant output, using thousands of signals per worker/task to separate "worst of the worst" from "best of the best," much as Google Search ranking does for web pages. He argues taste and post-training philosophy — not just data volume — explain why Claude pulled ahead on coding and writing for so long, and why models will become more differentiated (not commoditized) as labs' underlying values increasingly shape model behavior. His most pointed critique: he believes frontier labs are optimizing AI toward "slop" — chasing flawed, easily-gamed benchmarks (like LM Arena, which rewards flashy formatting and hallucination-masking length over accuracy) and engagement-maximizing behaviors (sycophancy, endless "improve this more?" loops) analogous to the harms he saw from social-media engagement optimization earlier in his career. He walks through the evolution of post-training (SFT → RLHF → rubrics/verifiers → RL environments) and explains RL environments as rich, game-like simulations of real work (e.g., a startup's Slack/Gmail/GitHub going down) used to train models on long-horizon, multi-step, ambiguous tasks that isolated benchmarks don't capture. He closes with a pointed anti-Silicon-Valley-playbook argument for founders: skip VC money, don't pivot for growth's sake, build the one thing only you could build, and prioritize following your own values over dashboard metrics.

## Key Points

- **Surge AI's growth**: $1B+ revenue in under 4 years, <100 people, fully bootstrapped, profitable from day one — achieved by deliberately avoiding VC funding, hype, and the "Silicon Valley game" (constant tweeting/PR), relying instead on word-of-mouth from researchers who understood and valued high-quality data.
- **Data quality reframed**: quality isn't a checklist (line count, keyword presence) but a genuinely rich, high-bar notion — akin to distinguishing a technically-correct poem from a Nobel-worthy one. Surge tracks thousands of signals per worker and task (background, expertise, keystroke patterns, review outcomes, downstream model performance) to identify both "worst of the worst" (spam-equivalent) and "best of the best" contributors, mirroring how Google Search ranks web pages.
- **Why Claude led on coding/writing for so long**: Chen attributes this partly to data choices (what to gather, from whom, in what proportions) and partly to "taste" in post-training — an inherently subjective, artistic judgment call about what good model behavior even looks like, not a purely technical optimization.
- **Benchmarks don't correlate well with real progress**: many popular benchmarks contain outright errors, and even accurate ones reward objectively-scored, "hill-climbable" tasks (e.g., IMO-style math) that diverge sharply from messy real-world ambiguity — "models can win IMO gold medals but still have trouble parsing PDFs."
- **[[LM Arena Gaming]]**: Chen's central critique of the industry's benchmark/engagement incentives — LM Arena rewards responses that look flashy (emojis, headers, length) over ones that are accurate, since casual voters skim rather than fact-check; Surge's own data shows adding markdown flourishes and lengthening responses is "the easiest way to climb" the leaderboard, even while accuracy degrades. He connects this to social-media engagement optimization (which produced clickbait/dark patterns) and worries AI chatbots are being optimized the same way — sycophancy, "you're absolutely right," endless unnecessary iteration — to maximize engagement rather than genuine usefulness.
- **How Surge actually measures model progress**: expert human annotators (domain specialists, not casual raters) have deep working conversations with models in their field and evaluate outputs rigorously (checking code, verifying physics, etc.) rather than skimming and picking whichever response "looks slickest" — a deliberately higher bar than casual online A/B comparisons.
- **AGI timeline**: Chen is on the longer end — expects models to automate roughly 80% of an average senior software engineer's job within 1-2 years, but says closing the remaining gap (90%, 99%, 99.9%) will take much longer, putting genuine AGI a decade or more away.
- **[[RL Environments]]**: rich, game-like simulations of real work (e.g., a startup's Slack, Gmail, GitHub, and AWS all interacting, then something breaks) used to train models on long-horizon, multi-step, ambiguous tasks — deliberately designed to expose weaknesses that isolated single-step benchmarks hide. Chen stresses the importance of full **trajectories** (not just final answers), since a model can reach a correct result through wasteful, inefficient, or reward-hacked paths that shouldn't be reinforced as-is.
- **Post-training evolution**: SFT (mimicking a master) → RLHF (ranking many candidate outputs) → rubrics/verifiers (detailed graded feedback) → RL environments (learning by doing, in simulated real-world settings) — framed as complementary layers of a broader "suite" of learning methods, not a strict replacement sequence, echoing the many different ways humans themselves learn a skill like writing.
- **Model differentiation, not commoditization**: Chen revises an earlier view that all frontier models would converge in capability and behavior; he now believes each lab's underlying values/objective-function choices (e.g., how many rounds of "let's make this email even better" a model should offer before just telling you to send it) will make models genuinely, durably different from one another — comparable to how Google, Facebook, and Apple would each build a fundamentally different search engine.
- **Underhyped**: in-chat "mini apps"/interactive artifacts (e.g., Claude generating a clickable send-this-message widget) as the next evolution beyond plain chat. **Overhyped**: vibe coding, which he expects to leave many codebases unmaintainable long-term.
- **Anti-Silicon-Valley-playbook founder advice**: don't chase VC money, don't pivot every two weeks for growth's sake, don't blitz-scale hiring, don't hire for resume-padding — build the one thing only you, given your specific background and convictions, could build, and accept that failing at something deep and novel beats succeeding at another generic "LLM wrapper" company. Cites ongoing research with VC [[Terrence Rohan]] interviewing early employees who spotted generational companies (OpenAI, Stripe) before they were obviously big, finding the common thread is founders/early believers with outsized conviction, not people chasing product-market fit wherever it leads.
- **Deeper mission**: frames Surge's work as helping customers define and measure their "dream objective function" for a model — analogous to deciding what kind of person you want a child to grow into, not just what test score they should get — arguing this is inherently harder (and more important) than optimizing easy, measurable proxies like clicks and engagement.
- Lightning round: recommends Ted Chiang's *Story of Your Life* (basis for the film *Arrival*), Camus's *The Myth of Sisyphus*, and Douglas Hofstadter's *Le Ton beau de Marot*; enjoyed the TV show *Travelers* and rewatching *Contact*; recently rode a Waymo for the first time ("it really felt like a living future"); describes his life philosophy as building/doing the thing only your specific background and interests could produce.
