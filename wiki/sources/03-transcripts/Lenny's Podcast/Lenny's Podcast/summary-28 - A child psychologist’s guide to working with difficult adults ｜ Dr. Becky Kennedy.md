## Core Summary

[[Lenny Rachitsky]] interviews [[Dr. Becky Kennedy]], clinical psychologist and CEO of [[Good Inside]], on the premise that the principles she teaches for raising resilient kids apply directly to leading "difficult" adults at work, because all humans — age one or eighty-five — share the same underlying needs and the same mechanism behind bad behavior: feelings that overpower the skills needed to manage them. The conversation walks through a set of named frameworks — the power of [[Power Of Repair|repair]], [[Connecting Before Correcting]], [[Behavior Vs Identity|"good inside" (separating behavior from identity)]], the [[Most Generous Interpretation]] (MGI), [[Sturdy Leadership|becoming a "sturdy" leader]], [[Boundaries Vs Requests|boundaries vs. requests]], and [[Resilience Over Happiness]] — each explained first in a parenting context and then explicitly translated into workplace leadership advice. Kennedy also describes Good Inside's business (app, live coaching, community, an AI chatbot named "Dr. GG," a second children's book called *Leave Me Alone*) and how she personally uses AI tools (Figma Make, Replit, Lovable, Claude, ChatGPT) to rapidly turn her product ideas into visual prototypes for her team. The episode is explicitly framed as bridging parenting advice and leadership advice, arguing that "most adults in the corporate environment are really just babies in disguise" (a framing credited to a prior podcast guest) — not because adults are literally childish, but because unmet needs produce the same kind of ineffective, escalating behavior at any age.

## Key Points

- **Repair over perfection**: the core relationship skill isn't avoiding mistakes but going back afterward to take responsibility, acknowledge impact, and say what you'll do differently — "perfect is creepy." Secure attachment is defined by the presence of repair, not the absence of rupture.
- **Connecting before correcting**: people (and kids) cooperate once they feel seen as a full person with their own priorities, not as an object to get something done; connection requires briefly dropping any agenda.
- **Good inside / behavior vs. identity**: separating a person's identity ("good inside") from their behavior (e.g., chronic lateness) defuses defensiveness and makes the actual behavior discussable; collapsing the two ("that person is lazy") is what makes conversations unproductive.
- **Most Generous Interpretation (MGI)**: a concrete tool for acting on "assume good intent" — actively generate the most generous plausible explanation for someone's behavior before intervening, which changes both your feelings toward the person and the intervention you choose.
- **Sturdy leadership**: illustrated with a turbulent-flight/pilot metaphor — a sturdy leader neither dismisses others' distress ("stop freaking out") nor hands over the decision to whoever is loudest ("does anyone want to fly the plane?"), but validates the feeling as real while calmly holding the decision.
- **Boundaries vs. requests**: a boundary is "what you tell someone else you will do," requiring nothing of the other person; if success depends on the other person's compliance, it was a request, not a boundary — most people who complain their boundaries aren't "respected" were actually making requests.
- **Resilience over happiness**: optimizing for a kid's (or employee's) immediate happiness/comfort builds fragility; resilience — the ability to tolerate a wide range of difficult experiences (disappointment, jealousy, hard feedback) — is what produces durable happiness later. Withholding hard feedback to avoid short-term discomfort (illustrated with a Kim Scott/*Radical Candor* anecdote about a fired employee) causes worse harm long-term.
- **"I believe you" + "I believe in you"**: a support formula for someone struggling — validate that the difficulty is real ("I believe you") while also holding a more capable vision of them than they can currently see for themselves ("I believe in you"); doing only the first collapses into the person's distress, doing only the second dismisses it.
- **Feelings overpower skills**: bad behavior at any age is a manifestation of a skills gap, not a moral failing — "kids are born with all the feelings and none of the skills to manage feelings." Punishment/shame don't teach the missing skill and widen the gap; boundaries plus explicit skill-teaching do.
- **Temperament framing**: "resilient rebels" (core fear = loss of control, resist any sense of being managed) and "deeply feeling kids" (core fear = being "too much," subject of her second book *Leave Me Alone*) are offered as recognizable adult-workplace personality patterns too.
- Kennedy describes her own AI workflow: she "vomits" ideas into tools like Figma Make to produce a full visual prototype (UX flow, copy, tone) rather than a verbal pitch, because that's how she thinks; she frames unstructured, high-volume prompting (not neatly pre-organized asks) as the effective way to use AI.
