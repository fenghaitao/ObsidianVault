## Core Summary

[[Lenny Rachitsky]] interviews [[Tomer Cohen]], outgoing longtime Chief Product Officer at [[LinkedIn]] (announced as leaving after 14 years around the time of this episode), about LinkedIn's "[[Full-Stack Builder Model]]" — a company-wide restructuring of product development that scraps traditional function-siloed teams (PM/design/engineering) in favor of individual "full-stack builders" who take an idea from research through launch themselves, aided by custom internal AI agents. Cohen frames the shift as a response to a fundamental problem: over years, at-scale companies like LinkedIn expanded every step of product development (research, design, review, launch) into many sub-steps, each individually justified but collectively producing enormous process and organizational complexity (multiple teams, codebases, and sprints just to ship a small feature). LinkedIn's own labor-market data shows the skills required for existing jobs will change by roughly 70% by 2030, which Cohen treats as forcing a first-principles rethink of how product work itself gets organized, not just which tools teams use. The program preserves five specifically human traits builders should focus on — vision, empathy, communication, creativity, and judgment (especially judgment) — while aggressively automating everything else via custom-built internal agents (trust, growth, research, analyst, coding, maintenance/QA agents), each requiring deep internal customization since off-the-shelf third-party AI tools consistently failed to work well against LinkedIn's own codebase, design systems, and proprietary context. Cohen stresses that the hardest, most underrated part of the rollout has been cultural change management (incentives, performance-review criteria, visible pilot wins, patience) — not the technology itself — and that counterintuitively, LinkedIn's top performers have adopted the new tools fastest, suggesting AI on its own tends to amplify already-strong builders rather than uniformly lifting everyone.

## Key Points

- **The core problem — process/organizational complexity**: at scale, every step of the product-development lifecycle (research, design, review, build, launch) gets subdivided into many individually-justified sub-steps (10-15 research sources, multiple review types, etc.), which in turn requires more specialized people to execute each sub-step — producing "process complexity" and "organizational complexity" that compound into a system where shipping a small feature can require multiple teams, codebases, and sprints.
- **[[Full-Stack Builder Model]]**: LinkedIn's response — empower individual "full-stack builders" (any function: PM, design, engineering, even business development) to take an idea to market end-to-end, working in small, fluid, cross-functional pods (not solo) rather than large siloed teams; explicitly modeled on a Navy SEALs analogy (broadly cross-trained operators organized around a mission, assembled and reassembled quickly).
- **Five preserved human traits** (everything else is targeted for automation): vision (a compelling stance on the future), empathy (deep understanding of unmet need), communication (aligning and rallying others), creativity (possibilities beyond the obvious — Cohen doesn't think AI is there yet), and — most emphasized — judgment (high-quality decision-making under ambiguity).
- **Three components of the rollout**: (1) **platform** — re-architecting LinkedIn's core stack so AI can reason over it, since no third-party tool (coding agents, Figma, etc.) worked off-the-shelf against LinkedIn's existing codebase/design system without deep customization; (2) **tools/agents** — a growing suite of custom internal agents (trust agent, growth agent, research agent, analyst agent, coding/maintenance/QA agents, a "product jammer" agent), each trained on LinkedIn-specific proprietary knowledge rather than general world knowledge, with roughly 50% of failed builds now auto-fixed by a maintenance agent; (3) **culture** — deliberately treated as the hardest and most important piece, requiring incentive/performance-review changes, visible pilot successes, and patience, since simply shipping good-enough tools doesn't guarantee adoption.
- **Data-curation lesson**: giving an agent unrestricted access to a full knowledge corpus ("here's the whole drive") performs badly and hallucinates; the team had to curate specific "golden examples" and weighted, high-signal datasets for each agent — directly paralleling Cohen's experience over a decade earlier hand-curating examples of "what is a good professional LinkedIn post" when rebuilding the LinkedIn feed from scratch.
- **Surprising finding — top talent adopts fastest**: LinkedIn's best performers were the earliest and most enthusiastic adopters of the new tools/workflow, suggesting (contra a common hope) that AI may primarily make already-great builders more effective rather than uniformly elevating weaker performers — reinforcing why change management (not just access) is essential for broader adoption.
- **Change-management tactics that worked**: tying tool adoption to performance reviews/promotion criteria and hiring bar; visible internal pilot wins (e.g., a partnerships lead building his own developer-portal connectors himself; a user researcher who used the tools to move into an open growth-PM role); a formal, selective "Associate Full-Stack Builder" program (replacing LinkedIn's traditional APM program) as a visible talent signal; sharing tool discoveries organically (Slack/Teams) rather than mandating specific tools top-down; deliberately not rushing a full company-wide rollout, to avoid diluting the program's perceived value.
- Cohen's advice to builders and leaders: don't wait for a formal reorg or official rollout to start working in a full-stack-builder mindset — "prove you're a full-stack builder in mindset before anything else"; invest seriously in the platform/tooling groundwork before expecting productivity gains; treat this as continuous, never-ending progress rather than a fixed end state.
- Lightning round: recommends *Why Nations Fail* (institutions determine national success, not culture/geography/religion), *Outlive* by Peter Attia (personalized "Medicine 3.0"), and *The Beginning of Infinity* by David Deutsch (progress requires genuine causal explanations, not just correlations); enjoys the Hebrew-language podcast "One Song" (deep dives on individual songs, similar to *Song Exploder*); praises voice-mode conversations with ChatGPT during commutes and Tesla's built-in Grok assistant; describes his life motto as "becoming is better than being" — valuing continuous growth over reaching any fixed state.
