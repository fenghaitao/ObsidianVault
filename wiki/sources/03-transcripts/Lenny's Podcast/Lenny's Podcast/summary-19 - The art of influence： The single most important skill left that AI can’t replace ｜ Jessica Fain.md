## Core Summary

[[Lenny Rachitsky]] interviews [[Jessica Fain]] ([[Box]], [[Slack]], [[Brightwheel]], now [[Webflow]]; former chief of staff to [[April Underwood]] and [[Tamar Yehoshua]]) on the art and science of influencing executives — a skill she argues is more, not less, valuable as AI compresses execution time. Her central mental model is the "[[Strobe Light Calendar Model]]": executives context-switch constantly and haven't been thinking about your problem since your last meeting, so you must actively re-center them rather than assume shared context. She distinguishes manipulative "politics" from genuine "[[Politics Vs Influence|influence]]" (increasing the odds good ideas survive through empathy, not ego), and gives concrete tactics: open meetings with 30-60 seconds of context-setting, present multiple options rather than one (referencing Barbara Minto's "[[Minto Pyramid Principle]]"), align pitches tightly to an executive's actual incentives, and ask "what led you to believe that?" instead of arguing past disagreement. She describes Slack-era practices like the "[[Customer Love Sprint]]" and "[[Stuart Plus Two More]]" design-review norm, and argues trust is built through delivered results plus "[[Shrinking The Change]]" (small, low-risk experiments, from the book *Switch*) rather than big asks. She closes arguing AI shifts PM leverage from execution/synthesis toward deciding what survives and building buy-in for it — and that the same influence playbook now applies directly to directing AI agents as teammates.

## Key Points

- "[[Strobe Light Calendar Model]]": executives are constantly context-switching and haven't been centering your problem — you must proactively re-establish context every meeting.
- "[[Politics Vs Influence]]": politics manipulates outcomes for personal gain; influence increases the odds good ideas survive through genuine curiosity and empathy toward stakeholders.
- Presentation tactics: don't lead with exhaustive proof-of-work (put it in an appendix); show multiple options considered, not just one; reference the "[[Minto Pyramid Principle]]" (recommendation first, then options, then evidence).
- Align pitches (and ongoing roadmap work) tightly to an executive's actual incentives/success metrics, not just your own team's goals.
- Ask "that's so interesting — what led you to believe that?" to co-create with disagreement rather than argue past it.
- "[[Customer Love Sprint]]" and "[[Stuart Plus Two More]]" — concrete Slack-era practices for aligning craft/culture with executive expectations while still preserving a team's own point of view.
- Trust is built through delivered results and "[[Shrinking The Change]]" (small, low-risk proofs of concept) rather than big asks — and proactively killing/deprioritizing your own ideas is one of the highest-trust-building moves available.
- On AI: PM leverage shifts from execution/synthesis (which AI now does well) toward deciding what survives and building organizational buy-in for it — "a golden age of product management," even as the traditional PM role changes. The same influence playbook (context-setting, incentive-alignment, curiosity) applies directly to directing AI agents as teammates.
- Views distribution and trust-building as durably valuable regardless of AI capability growth.
