## Core Summary

[[Dan Shipper]], CEO of [[Every]], returns to [[Lenny's Podcast]] a year after correctly predicting the rise of [[Claude Code]] to make a new set of predictions about how AI changes work over the next year. His central argument: work will bifurcate into (1) a company-wide "super agent" (accessed mostly via [[Slack]]) that people delegate tasks to, replacing the "everyone has their own agent" / parallel-org-chart model he originally expected, and (2) most individual knowledge work moving onto a computer-use surface like [[Codex]] or [[Claude Cowork]] that becomes the operating system for work, with a browser embedded inside the agent rather than an agent embedded inside a browser. This shift, in his view, lets SaaS tools survive and even thrive (agents multiply the number of "users" hitting them, and users bring their own tokens) rather than be replaced. He rejects both the "SaaS apocalypse" and the "AI job apocalypse" as overblown: models make "yesterday's human competence" cheap and commoditized, but every automated agent still needs an attentive human "gardening" it, so demand shifts toward roles like forward-deployed engineers, and he is especially bullish on product managers and full-stack designers who pair AI-driven building speed with product/design taste. He also predicts OpenAI's Codex has overtaken Claude Code as the leading agentic work surface, that CLIs as the primary interface are already fading in favor of GUIs, and that AI-generated writing (documents, plans, emails) will become normal and welcomed rather than stigmatized.

## Key Points

- Work bifurcates into: (1) one company-wide super-agent reached via Slack (Dan flipped from expecting personal/per-employee agents to a single shared agent, because agents "need a human who cares about them" and personal agents are too high-maintenance for most people to sustain) and (2) most work happening inside a computer-use agent surface (Codex/Claude Cowork) with an in-app browser, which becomes the de facto "operating system" for work.
- The "SaaS apocalypse is dumb" — Dan says he'd buy SaaS stocks; agents increase the number of effective SaaS users (and often run on the user's own AI tokens rather than the vendor's), improving SaaS margins rather than destroying the category.
- The "AI job apocalypse is not really a thing" — models commoditize "yesterday's human competence," but "automation is a lie" in the sense that every deployed agent still needs a human overseeing/maintaining it (echoing his earlier essay concept, "the allocation economy"); this creates durable new roles like the forward deployed engineer.
- Dan is "super bullish" on product managers and full-stack designers: both groups can now personally build (shipping pull requests, not just handing off specs), pairing craft/taste with AI-driven execution speed — while roles like sales and, so far, CEOs/executives look comparatively least changed.
- Introduces his own "senior engineer benchmark" (built on his product Proof, which he vibe-coded and then had two senior engineers independently rewrite): GPT-5.5 is the first model to score meaningfully higher (62/100 vs. ~30/100 for prior models, vs. high-80s/low-90s for a human senior engineer) because it's willing to rip out and rewrite bad code from first principles rather than just patch around it.
- Predicts Codex (OpenAI) has overtaken Claude Code as his own daily driver and, in his view, the leading edge of the agent-as-work-surface paradigm, though he expects the lead to keep changing hands ("horse race"); CLIs as the primary interface are already fading in favor of GUI-based agent surfaces for non-programmer work.
- Predicts AI-generated writing (strategy documents, quarterly plans, emails) will be increasingly normalized and preferred, as long as the human "stands behind" the content — contrasted with low-effort "slop" the sender doesn't understand or endorse.
