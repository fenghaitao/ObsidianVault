## Core Summary

[[Lenny Rachitsky]] interviews [[Jeanne DeWitt Grosser]], COO at [[Vercel]] (overseeing marketing, sales, customer success, revenue ops, and field engineering), who previously built Stripe's early sales team from the ground up as its CPO and started her career on the original Gmail team at [[Google]]. Grosser defines go-to-market (GTM) broadly as any function that touches a customer or makes a dollar, and argues AI is now driving a wave of consolidation across the roughly 17 historically hyper-specialized GTM roles back into a more integrated life cycle. Her central new concept is the "[[Go-To-Market Engineer]]" — a technical, often ex-sales-engineer hire who builds custom internal AI agents that replicate and then exceed the best practices of a company's top human performers on specific GTM workflows (lead qualification, outbound prospecting, deal coaching). At Vercel, one GTM engineer built a "lead agent" in six weeks that let the team shift 9 of 10 inbound SDRs to outbound work while holding conversion rates flat; a "lost-deal" analysis agent ("dealbot") built in two days now runs in real time against Gong call transcripts to surface coaching insights (e.g., revealing that a deal reported lost "on price" was actually lost because the team never reached the economic buyer). Grosser's other central framework is treating GTM itself as a product: mapping the full customer journey from first awareness to five-year retained customer, applying rigorous, multi-dimensional segmentation (not just company size, but growth potential, traffic/"promote" signals, and workload type) to know exactly who to target and how, and deliberately designing distinctive, high-value customer experiences (like a hands-on architecture whiteboarding session in Stripe's first sales call) rather than transactional, generic sales interactions. She closes with tactical advice on positioning around risk-avoidance rather than upside, sales compensation flexibility, and building sales teams that double as an extension of product/R&D.

## Key Points

- **[[Go-To-Market Engineer]]**: an emerging technical role (often filled by ex-sales-engineers or engineers-turned-salespeople) whose job is to break down GTM functions into discrete workflows and turn the ones AI can do well into agents, starting with the most "legible" (deterministic, easily-documented) workflows first (inbound lead qualification) before moving to harder ones (large-enterprise outbound prospecting, which will remain human-heavy longest).
- **Vercel's lead agent case study**: replaced most of a 10-person inbound SDR function with 1 person QA-ing an AI agent (the other 9 moved to outbound); took ~6 weeks and ~30% of one GTM engineer's time to build; held lead-to-opportunity conversion rate flat while cutting the number of touches needed to convert, since the agent responds instantly instead of leads sitting in a queue overnight.
- **Vercel's "dealbot"**: built from Gong call transcripts, Slack messages, and emails; evolved from a post-mortem "lost-deal" analysis tool (2 days to build) into a real-time in-Slack deal-coaching agent that flags things like "you're this far into the process and haven't reached an economic buyer yet." Running weekly retros on what the agent flags as GTM "bugs" (analogous to an engineering sprint) and shipping fixes to objection-handling and discovery content the following week.
- **Build vs. buy**: Grosser argues these agents are cheaper and easier to build in-house than expected (the lead agent costs ~$1,000/year to run, versus well over $1M in prior SDR salary) — largely because a company's own proprietary context/workflow is what makes an agent actually useful, and off-the-shelf tools tend to be narrow point solutions rather than integrated platforms at this stage of the market.
- **[[GTM As Product]]**: Grosser's framing, developed after noticing technical differentiation between competing products narrowing over time (illustrated by her own early Gmail experience) — if products converge on similar quality, the *experience of being sold to* becomes the differentiator. Concretely: map the full customer journey (first awareness through multi-year retention) the way a PM maps a product journey, and deliberately design distinctive moments (e.g., Stripe's first sales call being a hands-on payments-architecture whiteboarding session instead of a generic discovery quiz) rather than transactional, forgettable interactions.
- **Multi-dimensional segmentation**: beyond simple company-size buckets (SMB/mid-market/enterprise), Grosser layers in growth potential (critical for consumption-based pricing), "promote" signals like web-traffic rank (a smaller company with outsized traffic gets treated as enterprise), and workload/business-model type (B2B vs. B2C vs. platform/marketplace at Stripe; e-commerce vs. SaaS vs. crypto at Vercel) — each combination implying different buying content, product fit, and sales motion. Recommends starting segmentation work in a company's first 30 days, and treating it as a company-wide (not just GTM) discipline.
- **Buying psychology — pain avoidance over upside**: cites a stat that ~80% of customers buy to avoid pain/reduce risk rather than to chase upside; positioning around "how you'll be worse off / at risk if you don't act" resonates far more broadly (especially in enterprise) than "here's the exciting future we'll unlock together," which mostly lands with fellow founders. Echoes a related point from a separate episode with [[April Dunford]].
- **Sales-as-R&D**: Grosser's stated goal for a GTM org is to be "equal parts revenue-driving and R&D" — since a sales team may talk to more customers per week than an entire product research function, translating that raw feedback into structured signal (not just feature requests) makes GTM function as an extension of product management. Her litmus test for a strong account executive: put them in a room with 10 engineers, and it should take 10 minutes for the engineers to realize they aren't a PM.
- **PLG has a ceiling**: product-led growth remains a strong default starting motion for non-enterprise-first products, but self-serve flows rarely close large deals; companies that delay building a real sales motion (inbound-to-outbound) hit a growth wall. Pricing should be treated as its own product discipline — Grosser cites Vercel's shift from a bundled SaaS-like price to an unbundled Pro/Enterprise split (moving startup-relevant features out of the Enterprise SKU into self-serve) as a major PLG growth driver.
- **Hiring/comp**: values a "diversified portfolio" sales team mixing traditional sales backgrounds with consulting/banking backgrounds, since each teaches the other (quantitative/analytical rigor vs. core sales skill); flags that rigid, annually-locked sales compensation plans conflict with the need to pivot quickly in a fast-changing AI product landscape (Vercel's AI Cloud product didn't exist when this year's sales plan was written).
