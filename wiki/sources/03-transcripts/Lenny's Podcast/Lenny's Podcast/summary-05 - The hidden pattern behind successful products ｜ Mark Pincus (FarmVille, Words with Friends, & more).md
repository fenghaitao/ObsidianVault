## Core Summary

[[Mark Pincus]], founder of [[Zynga]] and author of the book "[[Life at the Speed of Play]]," lays out [[Proven Better New]] — his core product framework: human instincts are right ~95% of the time but the ideas built on top of them are wrong ~75% of the time, so founders should master a "proven" pattern already working for a specific platform/audience, refine it into a "better" version that 10-out-of-10 existing users would want, and only then layer on one small, likely-to-fail "new" idea. He argues that copying is not shameful if it's in service of the end user rather than peer approval ("burn your resume"), and illustrates the framework with stories from Zynga (FarmVille, Words With Friends, CityVille, Mafia Wars, Zynga Poker), plus outside case studies (Craigslist, Slack, Angry Birds vs. OMG Pop's Draw Something, Bolt.new, the iPhone). A second major thread is "[[Kill Hope Before Hope Kills You]]": teams should distinguish belief (evidence-based confidence) from hope (confidence without basis), build a "[[Maximum Launchable Product]]" instead of a hope-driven MVP, and use AI to run many fast, cheap product-failure experiments rather than one slow, expensive bet. Pincus also discusses why being less ambitious at the outset is paradoxically the path to the biggest ideas, Zynga's obsession with [[Day 365 Retention]] and a proprietary [[Active Social Network]] metric over virality, why consumer/social has "lost the adrenaline" and needs a new "[[Cocktail Party Distribution]]" moment (with AI agents as a possible "[[Social Membrane]]" broker), and management/parenting philosophy ([[Make Everyone A CEO]], [[Close To The Metal]], the [[Teaching Hospital Model]] for mentorship, and raising kids for a post-knowledge-work world).

## Key Points

- **Proven Better New**: instincts are ~95% right, ideas built on them are ~75% wrong; isolate your "innovation zone," master what's already proven on that exact platform/audience, make it demonstrably better (10/10 existing users would switch), then add one small new/novel element — accepting that the "new" part will probably fail.
- Sid Meier's social-civilization Facebook game reportedly died on arrival because its first-time-user-experience wasn't a perfect copy of proven onboarding patterns, despite great "new" game design — proof that skipping "proven" sinks even great innovation.
- "Moral arbitrage" (credited to Peter Thiel's framing): founders resist copying because school taught them it's cheating, which leaves the copying opportunity more available to those with less ego; ambition should be defined by the end consumer's experience (e.g., "nurses in Indiana" for FarmVille), not peer respect.
- Craig Newmark spent two years adding photos to Craigslist listings, prioritizing that users could still scan text quickly — an example of a "world-class product maker" perfecting a small, non-obvious "better."
- Being less ambitious at the start is the path to the most ambitious outcomes: Tribe failed by trying to do everything at once; Zynga succeeded by starting "embarrassingly small" (a Facebook poker game). Rovio needed 45 different game attempts (pure innovation, high variance) to hit Angry Birds; OMG Pop pivoted to perfectly copying Zynga's Words With Friends turn-based mechanic to make Draw Something a hit (Proven Better New, lower variance).
- "Kill hope before hope kills you": hope is confidence without basis; belief is evidence-based. Best product makers "collect winnings, they don't make bets." A Maximum Launchable Product (not a hope-driven Minimum Viable Product) is a product you already believe, based on evidence, will be a hit.
- AI should be used to build many cheap, fast "wrong" products for signal/testing (a "testing machine, failure machine") rather than one polished but slow bet — e.g., Zynga turned an ad-budget line item for a FarmVille expansion into an in-game "coming soon" test that generated both marketing signal and $19M in early-access key sales.
- Zynga's real edge wasn't virality but retention: it was reportedly the only consumer company tracking Day 365 retention, plus a proprietary "Active Social Network" (ASN) metric — going from 0 to 1 round-trip social interaction correlated with an 80% chance of returning next month, and reaching ASN 4 correlated with 80% chance of playing 22 of the next 30 days.
- Consumer/social apps have "lost the adrenaline" (people are proud to announce they quit Instagram); Pincus argues for finding or building a new "cocktail party" — a place with organic lead-generation/productivity value — and speculates AI agents could broker relationships via a "social membrane" of shared context and trust.
- Management philosophy: "make everyone a CEO" (give people a hill to take and full operating control within a budget/plan so they self-motivate and stop needing management); "close to the metal" (best product CEOs, e.g., Steve Jobs on carpet choices, stay in the pixel-level details rather than delegating core UX decisions); the "teaching hospital" model of grooming a "tech assistant" who shadows the founder for 6–12 months (cited via Andy Jassy's path under Jeff Bezos at Amazon).
- On parenting in the AI era: Pincus says mass-produced, knowledge-worker-oriented education is ending, so he teaches his kids critical thinking, generative (not consumptive) online behavior, and to interrogate authority figures' agendas rather than memorize answers.
