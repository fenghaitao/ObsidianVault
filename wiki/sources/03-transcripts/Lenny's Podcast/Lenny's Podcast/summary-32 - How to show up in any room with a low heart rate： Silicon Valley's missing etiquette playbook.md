## Core Summary

Sam Lessin (partner at Slow Ventures, former VP of Product at Facebook, two-time founder) argues that etiquette is a vastly underrated skill in Silicon Valley — not about stuffy rules, but about showing up in any room with a low heart rate, projecting genuine confidence, and building trust. He frames etiquette as the hidden social infrastructure that makes the difference between success and missed opportunities, especially for young founders who've been encouraged to be abrasive. The conversation covers practical etiquette across introductions, conversations, hygiene, dress, dining, scheduling, communication, meetings, and exiting, all anchored in the principles of abundance mindset, giving more than you take, and leaving people wanting more.

## Key Points

- **The core framing**: Etiquette is "a skill for how to show up in a room with a low heart rate." It's about projecting calm, self-confidence, and an abundance mindset rather than transactional scarcity.
- **Why etiquette matters now**: In an era of commoditized software and AI fear, trust is the differentiator. Understanding how to meet people where they're at, mirror expected behaviors, and build genuine relationships is critical for doing business.
- **The "funny but useful" intersection**: Sam's philosophy for content and teaching — humor cuts through the noise, but there must be a deeper truth. Teaching Silicon Valley people etiquette is inherently funny, but the value is real.
- **Introductions**: Be early (5-10 minutes, not 30). Have a firm but not crushing handshake. Repeat names back to show you're making an effort. If someone else is late, don't make them feel bad. Make eye contact — the effort matters as much as the execution. Introduce your partner first. Use "great to see you" instead of "nice to meet you" when you're unsure if you've met before. The "let it hang" trick: when you can't remember someone's name, say "I want to introduce you to [partner]" and let the other person fill in.
- **Conversations**: Welcome people into the conversation inclusively. Ask questions but don't turn it into an inquisition — think ping-pong, not singles. Match vocabulary to the person you're speaking with. Leave them wanting more. Have a few crowd-pleasing stories ready. Know how to wind down gracefully — bring someone else into the conversation or use "I'm going to grab a drink" (not an invitation to follow).
- **Hygiene**: Don't smell like anything — your scent should not be noticeable in any direction. Don't be covered in schmutz. Dress appropriately for the room.
- **Dress**: Dress one level up (not two or three). Fit matters more than brand — a well-fitting $20 shirt beats a misfitting $500 shirt. Don't show up as a startup founder with a Rolex. If you're unsure about the dress code, just ask — it shows confidence, not weakness. You can reduce formality (take off a jacket) but can't easily increase it.
- **Dining**: Tip well (20% minimum). Don't order the most expensive thing on the menu. Don't order first — let the host set the tone. Within reason, always offer to pay (you should be turned down). If you're out with someone very well-known, tip like crazy. Give the sommelier a taste if you order something nice. B for bread (left), D for drinks (right). Napkin goes in your lap. Knife blade faces inward.
- **Humor**: Make fun of yourself, not others. Have a ranked file of jokes from least to most offensive. Humor is the ultimate demonstration of comfort in a social space — but a joke nobody laughs at is a huge risk. Don't be remembered as only the comedian.
- **Scheduling etiquette**: The default should not be Calendly. If you're the less senior or less busy person, let the other person tell you when they're free and make it work. Give real options, and one of the first three should work. If you reschedule, you need to be even more accommodating. Respect time zones. Most importantly: deeply respect EAs, PAs, and anyone helping the other person — this is the number one way to look classless if you don't.
- **Communication**: Proofread. Get to the point quickly. Assume the reader is busy. Keep emails short — a 10-paragraph email is like a monologue. Be cautious with emojis — they're a step of familiarity and can be misread. Think about email order: who's in the To line vs CC line, and the order of names signals priority. Never BCC then reply all.
- **Meeting etiquette**: Arrive 10-15 minutes early (not an hour). Start with small talk as a "TCP/IP handshake" to establish the connection. For virtual meetings: camera on, dress appropriately, have an appropriate background (close your closet, make your bed). Clean up after yourself — offer to put your coffee cup away.
- **Exiting and leaving**: Stand when people leave the table. Stand to shake hands. Follow up with gratitude — send thank-you notes. Don't make a production of leaving — the Irish goodbye is often the best goodbye in large groups. Don't take calls during goodbyes.
- **The TL;DR**: Remember the goal of all etiquette is building trust and projecting genuine confidence. Always maintain an abundance mindset. Remember you are worthy and have nothing to prove. It's okay to ask questions. Keep your heart rate low.
