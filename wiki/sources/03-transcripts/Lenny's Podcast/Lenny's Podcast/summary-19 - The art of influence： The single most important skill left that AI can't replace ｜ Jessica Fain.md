# Summary: The art of influence： The single most important skill left that AI can't replace ｜ Jessica Fain

## Core Thesis

**Influence is the single most important skill for product leaders that AI can't replace.** As AI commoditizes execution, the leverage shifts from _doing the work_ to _deciding what work survives_ and getting others to buy in. Jessica Fain, drawing from her experience as Chief of Staff to two CPOs at Slack and as a product leader at Box, Slack, Brightwheel, and Webflow, argues that product managers misunderstand how executives make decisions. Instead of centering their own desires, PMs must apply curiosity and empathy to their executives — treating them as users to understand — and align their pitches with what the exec is measured on.

## Key Topics

### 1. The Executive's Calendar as a Strobe Light
- Executives have insanely fragmented calendars — they wake up at 8am with a huge list of urgent things, bouncing from finance to interviews to legal to product reviews.
- "The executive coming into that session hasn't thought about you since. They may not have gone to the bathroom today."
- Execs don't have the time, energy, or wherewithal to center your problems. You must help them get into the right mindset.
- **Tactic:** Spend 30 seconds at the top of a meeting: why are we here, what happened last time, why is this important, what are the goals.

### 2. Aligning Incentives: What Are They Measured On?
- Execs are optimizing for a global maximum, not your local maximum.
- Instead of asking "What's top of mind?" (which has become a "trope"), ask spicier questions: "What is the board pushing you on?" "What are the key inputs to your success?"
- Execs want to be successful and good at their jobs too. Connect your pitch to what they're measured on — their OKRs, board pressures, strategic goals.
- In the best case, your incentives at a team level align with theirs. If not, you're in misalignment from the start.

### 3. Curiosity and Empathy with Executives
- "As product managers, one of our best sets of skills is curiosity and empathy and trying to understand our users. But the moment that we're talking to an executive, we forget those skills."
- Treat your executive as your key user. Apply the same discovery-interview mindset.
- **Key phrase:** "That's so interesting. What led you to believe that?" — disarms the executive, shows genuine curiosity about their reasoning.
- Don't go in seeking approval. Go in to learn, strengthen your plan, and co-create with the executive.
- Execs are good at _seeming_ certain because they must make fast decisions with little information. But they may be learners with growth mindsets.

### 4. "It's Not My Fault, But It Is My Problem"
- Annie Pearl's mantra: "It's not my fault, but it is my problem."
- Lenny's version: "It's your fault if the leaders didn't buy into your ideas."
- You can't blame the exec for not seeing your vision. The responsibility to influence them is yours.
- But this isn't about being a "yes man." You get paid to have an opinion and be a domain expert. The exec is looking for you to be the deepest person in the room.

### 5. Presenting Options (Goldilocks Approach)
- **Mistake:** Giving only one option. It makes you look like you haven't considered alternatives.
- **Solution:** Present three options — the classic pricing-strategy approach. Show the Goldilocks middle option as your recommendation.
- Demonstrate that you've considered what the exec might think you missed.
- Have backup options ready in an appendix or Figma draft.
- **"Stuart plus two more":** After Stuart Butterfield gave design direction, the team would deliver exactly what he asked for plus two more versions they felt good about, creating a forum for debate.

### 6. The Mento Pyramid and Communication Styles
- **Mento Pyramid:** Start with your recommendation, then the options explored, then the evidence — not the other way around.
- But execs have different communication preferences. Some hate PowerPoint, some only want data, some want a doc with 10 minutes of quiet reading time.
- **Advice for execs:** Just tell people your preferred format. It's incredibly helpful.
- Ask people around the exec (EA, chief of staff, colleagues who've successfully pitched) what works.

### 7. "Shrinking the Change" and Building Trust
- From the book _Switch_ (change management): If something seems scary, make it smaller — an experiment, a one-week proof of concept.
- **Red teaming:** Take an outsider's perspective to your idea. Stress-test it.
- **Build trust by killing things:** Deprioritizing or killing your own projects shows you have the same aligned incentives as the executive. It's a very senior way of thinking.
- **Desire for certainty:** Instead of needing certainty about the outcome, create certainty about the next check-in point. "I don't know if this will work. We'll check in on X date."

### 8. Picking Up on Executive Threads
- Execs often leave subtle breadcrumbs of opinion — "I wonder if..." or "Have you considered..."
- The best people take the bait. They follow the thread quickly.
- **Example:** When Rachel Woolen mentioned design reviews would need rethinking, Kev (head of design) had a Loom with a framework ready within an hour.
- **Counterexample:** Tamar Yehoshua asked four times to see top 10 use cases. When the fourth time came, she got frustrated because no one had picked up on the cue.
- **Calibration question:** "How strongly do you feel about this?" or "Does this trump our current priorities?"

### 9. Think Like a CPO
- To be more senior, expand your viewpoint beyond your own feature/team. Think about the whole ecosystem, the whole organization, the whole industry.
- Connect your work back to business outcomes. Don't just say "our KR is to ship this." Explain why it matters — what user/business outcome it drives.
- This is how you get seen as a strategic thinker, not just a tactical executor.
- **Example:** Jeff Weinstein at Stripe constantly thinks about Stripe as a business, not just his own product area.

### 10. Constraints and Asking for What You Need
- Execs are not bound by the same budget, headcount, and timeline constraints you feel. They can move people, elevate projects, kill projects.
- If an exec's ask seems unreasonable, come back with: "Here's what I can do with today's resourcing. Here's the 10x case. Here's what I need to be successful."
- Don't miss the opportunity to ask for what you need to be successful.

### 11. AI's Impact on Influence
- We are entering a "golden age of product management" — not of product managers, but of product management.
- As execution plummets in complexity, the leverage shifts from doing the work to deciding what work survives and getting others to buy in.
- PMs who made their careers on being the most type-A, Gantt-chart-master, best note-taker will be displaced by AI.
- The core skills that remain: having interesting ideas grounded in user empathy, curiosity, testing, iteration, and influencing people to fund not just V1 but V2, V3, V4.
- **Strategy clarity becomes 10x more important.** When building happens faster, mistakes compound faster. Clear strategy anchors empowered teams.
- Agents as colleagues: we are all "directors of work" now. We need to codify what's important, set guardrails, and train agents on our product philosophy.

### 12. The Customer Love Sprint
- At Slack, after hearing that they'd lost their mojo around product craft, Jessica implemented a two-week "Customer Love Sprint."
- Engineers picked what they worked on. The only rule: ship something good for users.
- Result: 65 improvements shipped in one sprint. Spoke directly to what executives believed was Slack's differentiation.
- Tied the work to the exec team's strategic beliefs about what made Slack special.

### 13. Authenticity in Influence
- Influence is not one personality type. You can be shy, introverted, data-oriented, or technical and still communicate authentically.
- Jessica got early feedback to "show up more like a man" — more aggressive, more decisive. She learned that who she is is her superpower.
- "I don't need to be like someone else. What I need to do is think about what I want to accomplish and how I want to grow and do that in a way that is true to me."

### 14. Influence vs. Politics
- **Politics** = manipulating outcomes and people for your own gain.
- **Influence** = increasing the odds that your good ideas survive.
- When people frame influence as "icky" or "political," they're missing the point. It's about learning, empathy, and building great products together.
- "If you don't respect that person, if you don't believe they know something you don't, you need to quit."
