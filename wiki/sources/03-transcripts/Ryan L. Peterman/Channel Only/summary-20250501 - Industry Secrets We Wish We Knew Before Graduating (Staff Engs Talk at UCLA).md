## Core Summary

Ryan Peterman and Ricky, both staff engineers at Meta/Google, give a Q&A talk to UCLA students covering the unwritten rules of succeeding in the software engineering industry. They explain the engineering ladder from junior to staff, emphasize that promotions come from demonstrating next-level behaviors rather than just doing more current-level work, and stress that soft skills — asking questions, writing, self-advocacy, and visibility — matter as much as coding ability. They also discuss big tech vs. startups (high expected value vs. high variance), how to increase luck through agency, the prevalence of imposter syndrome, and the importance of being a multifaceted person beyond work.

## Key Points

- Engineering levels progress from junior (tasks with help) to mid-level (independent tasks) to senior (given an area, creates tasks) to staff (finds the areas); senior is the terminal level for most.
- Promotions are earned by demonstrating next-level behaviors, not by excelling at current-level work; talk to your manager constantly about what the next level requires.
- Internship success is measured by three factors: productivity (speed of project completion), code quality (decreasing review revisions), and initiative (proposing improvements beyond the project).
- Asking questions aggressively is the single biggest differentiator between high and low performers early in a career; silence and struggling alone are the fastest path to failure.
- Luck is part opportunity and part preparation; you can increase your luck surface area by taking initiative (e.g., applying broadly, grinding LeetCode, leaving bad situations).
- Big tech offers high expected value and prestige; startups offer high variance and potential moonshots; the speakers recommend starting in big tech for the brand equity and learning best practices.
- Imposter syndrome is nearly universal — both speakers experienced it even after multiple promotions — and the best response is to focus on learning as much as possible.
- Impact is defined as concrete, measurable outcomes (revenue, latency, user growth), not lines of code; a one-line change with 20% improvement beats thousands of meaningless lines.
- After doing good work, you must make it visible — through writing, presenting, or manager advocacy — or it effectively didn't happen.
- An MBA is not needed for engineering management; data shows comparable career outcomes with and without one.
- Work is not everything; the speakers' happiest moments came from life outside work, and they advise being a multifaceted person so that a layoff doesn't destroy your identity.
- AI is likely to augment rather than replace entry-level software engineers in the short to medium term; someone still needs to direct and verify the AI's output.
