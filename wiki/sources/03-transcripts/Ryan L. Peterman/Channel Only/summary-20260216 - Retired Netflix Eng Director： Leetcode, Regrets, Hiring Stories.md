## Core Summary

David Ronca, a retired engineering director at Netflix and Meta, tells an unfiltered 36-year career story: Netflix's "no brilliant jerks" culture and freedom-and-responsibility norms that contrasted with the 24/7 expectations of earlier jobs, a hiring philosophy grounded in foundational engineering judgment rather than LeetCode, the hard lesson of building trust as an externally hired senior leader, and a colon-cancer scare that permanently reshaped his view of work-life balance.

## Key Points

- Netflix's early culture was revolutionary: they "don't hire brilliant jerks," valued results in an 8-hour day over 24/7 work, and Patty McCord personally interviewed (and blessed) every hire in the early days.
- The culture memo ("don't hire brilliant jerks," top-of-market pay, the keepers test, freedom and responsibility) was aspirational — "it's what we aspire to be" — and, he argues, did not scale as the company grew.
- Netflix started with a single software-engineering level and no individual credit ("Netflix won," not named individuals); this under-recognition of great engineers is what he believes eventually pushes best people out.
- Compensation ran on "personal top of market": you could bring a competing offer and get matched; without levels, this became unsustainable and un-rationalizable — he argued (unsuccessfully) for adding levels before he left.
- On hiring, he trusts foundational engineering ability — understanding complex systems, strong technical intuition, and making decisions without enough data — over LeetCode, which "doesn't tell you much about an engineer"; this instinct is, in his view, innate like musical talent, and "you interview to the person, not to the process."
- His favorite hire story: a civil engineer from wastewater treatment who had never written code, but whose two-hour whiteboard of a sewage-treatment state machine showed "a damn good engineer" they could teach to code; he later made a similar call hiring a train-automation engineer everyone else passed on.
- On levels he advises "when in doubt, go low": coming in under-leveled and executing up earns ratings, bonuses, and RSU refreshers, while an over-leveled hire is nearly impossible to fix.
- He recruited Netflix/Meta encoding scientist Ioannis Katsavounidis, whose work produced content-based encoding and convex hull encoding (the industry's quality-per-bandwidth model) — his pick for the strongest engineer he has worked with.
- Joining Meta in 2019 to lead an already-well-functioning ~55-person video processing team, he initially tried to drive change too fast and was told "you've been here two weeks" — the lesson: step in and "shut up and work on building trust" first, then move the team.
- During COVID, every day became "New Year's Eve" for video traffic (his team historically planned all year for the NYE spike); the world sustained itself on Facebook Live and WhatsApp, which reframed Meta's reach — ~3.5 billion users vs Netflix's ~300 million top-5% users.
- He values Meta's PSC/calibration process ("the most dreaded and my favorite time of year") for turning individual credit into documented, argued, on-paper impact — a stark contrast with Netflix's credit culture.
- His colon-cancer diagnosis (stage 3, 25% five-year survival) after years of 24/7 startup work taught him work-life balance is non-negotiable; a poster at Meta — a rocking horse captioned "don't mistake motion for progress" — sums up his view.
- The book that stuck with him: "12 Secrets to Microsoft Success," which introduced betting the company, good failure ("failure can be success... it's how you fail, what you learn, and what you bring forward"), hiring the top 5%, dogfooding (Bill Gates forcing the company onto NT Server), and a company-wide hiring freeze to kill low-value projects.
- Career regrets/advice: he wasted his first six years at failing large companies; engineers should chase hard problems, try a startup, work for visionary leaders who can also execute, bake in work-life balance, and start financial planning on day one.
