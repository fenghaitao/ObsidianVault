## Core Summary

A panel discussion with engineers from Netflix, Airbnb, Amazon, Meta, and Google on how to get promoted to Staff Engineer (IC6) at FAANG companies. The panelists cover how to identify high-impact projects worth investing time in, how to have explicit promotion conversations with managers, how to evaluate which teams and orgs provide the best vehicle for promotion, and practical advice on visibility, work hours, internal brand, and navigating big tech promotion politics. The discussion emphasizes that infrastructure teams tend to offer more leveraged promotion paths at senior+ levels, that asking for promotion early (with openness to feedback) has no downside, and that self-advocacy and visibility are non-negotiable for advancement.

## Key Points

- Staff Engineer is the first level where you must be a leader as an individual contributor, and not everyone achieves it; the promotion is challenging and often decided by committee, not just your manager.
- The key skill for senior engineers is developing the "muscle" to identify which projects have sufficient scope and impact before investing time — look at org incentives, published team plans, and metrics.
- Being explicit with your manager about wanting promotion is critical; half of people ask too early and half ask too late, but asking early has no downside as long as you can receive and act on feedback.
- Infrastructure teams tend to have more natural leverage and more senior promotion opportunities than product teams, because the technology itself multiplies impact across many engineers.
- Panelists reported working 50-60 hours per week during their staff promotion push, though one noted force-multiplying through people leadership can achieve the same impact in fewer hours.
- Visibility and internal brand matter more than seniority for getting high-scope projects; being known as someone who gets things done is what opens doors.
- Titles outside big tech are often inflated; getting "down-leveled" when entering FAANG is more about correct leveling against scope, and the compensation increase often makes the title drop irrelevant.
- Equity compensation at higher levels shifts toward stock, creating geometric (not linear) compensation growth, but also carries risk tied to company stock performance.
