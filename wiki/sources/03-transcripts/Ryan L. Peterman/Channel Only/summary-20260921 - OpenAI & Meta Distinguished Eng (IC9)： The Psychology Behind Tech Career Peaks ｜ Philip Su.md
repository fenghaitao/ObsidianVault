## Core Summary

Philip Su's second appearance with Ryan L. Peterman moves past how he reached IC9 to the psychology of peaking: every career has an asymptote — a terminal level you eventually hit with decades of work still ahead. He traces why promotions stopped motivating him (the quantifiable "grades" effect of levels faded, the nature of senior work shifted toward meetings and influence he enjoyed less, and rising expectations met his honest self-appraisal), which led him to a voluntary demotion from IC9 to IC7. Philip says he would have been happier "titrating out" promotions across his whole career rather than speedrunning to the top in the first third, and he grounds long-term meaning in regular socialization and in knowing what you will do the day after you reach your biggest goal. His closing advice is to read Oliver Burkeman's 4,000 Weeks and to make explicit the family/career trade-offs he too often chose in the employer's favor.

## Key Points

- Every career has an asymptote — a terminal level you cannot grow past, often reached with 20-30 years of work left ahead.
- Early in his career, promotions were one of the few quantifiable feedback signals (like school grades), which drove his ambition through his 20s and early 30s.
- Beyond IC6 the nature of the work changes: less direct coding, more meetings, team direction, deciding whether the product is right, and influencing other orgs — "meta work" Philip enjoyed less.
- Philip stopped chasing levels before E8/E9; both promotions were surprises and consequences of work he already found interesting (he did not even know Facebook had levels until his boss announced his E8 promotion).
- He sought a voluntary demotion from IC9 to IC7; the hard parts were knowing he once could do more and the "wistful" feeling of watching former juniors rise past him.
- As Facebook grew from ~500 engineers to tens of thousands, Philip's generalist skills lost value relative to specialists, who survive better in large companies.
- On work intensity he would have "titrated out" promotions more slowly, and wishes he had recognized that hard work was buying later freedom.
- His two-part advice: plan "the day after" you reach your biggest goal, and prioritize regular socialization (work is "mandatory socialization"); he recommends the book 4,000 Weeks.
