## Core Summary

Igor, a senior staff (L7/E7) engineer across Google, Cruise, and Meta, honestly recounts asking Meta for a demotion — which Meta could not grant for an IC in the same job family — and returning to Google one level lower (L6) because he preferred coding, debugging, and mentoring over E7 leadership. He shares a candid account of senior-IC ramp-up, impostor syndrome, Meta-versus-Google culture, and why senior (E5/L5) has the best quality of life.

## Key Points

- At Meta, high-level hires are judged against other senior staff in PSC, and Meta had begun managing out the lowest ~10% performers — leaving at most a year to reach parity with organizational old-timers.
- Describes ramp-up as climbing from "zero" (knowing less than a two-week-old intern) back up the ladder; he judged he only reached ~E6 in his time at Meta, not E7.
- Asked management to drop a level and learned Meta has no process for demotion within the same job category; he did not push further because Google was his comfort zone.
- Ramps at Cruise (smaller, less crowded, easier to carve out space) succeeded, while Meta's senior space felt crowded; he regrets over-investing in reading docs instead of getting hands dirty.
- Senior-IC job mobility is riskier than it looks; people rarely share the failure stories, only the successes.
- Meta culture: artificial, aggressive deadlines with constant leadership status updates that people eventually dismiss ("the PE teacher is yelling but the kids are still playing"); Google (a decade ago) reserved real deadline pressure for genuine exceptions.
- Returning to Google at L6 (from L7) had no standard hiring process — it took recruiter work; he had to convince them he would accept lower compensation, and received a coding-interview exception.
- Attributes his willingness to share the demotion story to privilege: no visa dependence, spouse's health insurance, grown kids, and no mortgage.
- His Google L7 promotion came from leading ads machine-learning training infrastructure onto first-generation TPUs, including an "infinite-speed TPU" bottleneck test and ordering chips ~18 months ahead (usually under-ordering).
- Best quality of life: senior (E5/L5) — shielded by the team's TL and managers, with the least pressure; he would settle at E6 rather than E7.
- On impostor syndrome: don't compare yourself against a team in which each person is best at one thing; he judged himself only "partially" E7 across dimensions.
- Mentoring: don't underestimate interns — one intern he hosted later became that team's manager.
- Became disillusioned with Google's "don't be evil" idealism after it shut the Atlanta office with little empathy; ultimately the bottom line drives decisions.
- Advice to younger self: work on what matters — ask whether the company really benefits from what you are doing, and switch projects if not.
