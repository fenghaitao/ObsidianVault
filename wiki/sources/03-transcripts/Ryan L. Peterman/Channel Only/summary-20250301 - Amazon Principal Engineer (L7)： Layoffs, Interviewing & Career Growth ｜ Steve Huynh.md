## Core Summary

Ryan L. Peterman interviews Steve Huynh, a former Amazon Principal Engineer (L7) with 18 years at the company, covering his unconventional path from English/Creative Writing major to top-tier IC, Amazon's interviewing and performance management culture, and the career progression from SDE1 to Principal. Steve argues that behavioral interviews are the most underappreciated part of the hiring process—they serve as the "poker" round that determines leveling and fit, while coding and system design are merely necessary but not sufficient anti-fees. He also provides an insider's perspective on Amazon's stack ranking processes, the difficulty of the senior-to-principal leap (which he describes as effectively skipping a level), and the importance of proactive career management, including asking managers directly about expectations and making explicit decisions rather than drifting.

## Key Points

- Behavioral interviews have an outsized impact on hiring decisions and leveling; Amazon will reject candidates with strong coding and system design if behavioral answers are poor
- The interview prep industry over-indexes on coding and system design while neglecting the behavioral side of the interview
- Steve broke into Amazon without a CS degree (English/Creative Writing major) through networking—a friend from AP Computer Science referred him for a contract Support Engineer role
- Getting a job requires two things: opportunity (unevenly distributed) and preparedness (within your control); you need both to align
- The job market today has very low demand for junior engineers but extraordinary demand for senior and above; competition exists at the pre-qualification/resume stage, not the interview stage
- "English is the top programming language" — the ability to communicate, package your experience, and tell a good story is the highest-leverage activity
- Instead of going broad (learning many surface-level technologies), engineers should go deep in one area
- AI is an amplifier: it can take a non-coder from 0 to 1, and a 1x engineer to 10x, but cannot yet take someone from 0 to 10x
- Amazon's SDE1 to SDE2 promotion is about independence; SDE2 to SDE3 is about ownership; SDE3 to Principal is about cross-team influence and effectively doing two jobs
- Amazon skips a staff level—the SDE3 to Principal gap is like jumping two levels, which is why so many senior engineers fail or leave
- The most common failure mode for senior-to-principal: engineers focus on principal-level work and neglect their senior responsibilities, then get flagged as low-performing at SDE3
- Amazon's performance management has shifted from soft guidance (~5-6% managed out) to a harder mandate, and without backfills, the repeated cuts start affecting solid performers
- Steve's best managers were principles-based and willing to think long-term; the worst felt like they "fell up" through luck rather than competence
- The biggest career regret is deferring decisions—staying put by default rather than making an explicit choice about whether to leave a bad situation
- The advice to his younger self: stop seeking external validation and people-pleasing; examine whether you're climbing the ladder because you want to, or because the ladder was put in front of you
