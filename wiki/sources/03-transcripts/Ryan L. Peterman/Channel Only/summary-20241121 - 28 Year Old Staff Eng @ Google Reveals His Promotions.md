## Core Summary

Ricky (aka "finding Ricky"), a Staff Engineer at Google who reached that level by age 28, shares the detailed arc of his career from new grad (L3) to Staff (L6) across three promotions over approximately seven years. He explains the specific behavioral shifts required at each level — independence for L3→L4, scope ownership and becoming the go-to person for L4→L5, and challenging the status quo with cross-team influence for L5→L6 — while emphasizing bottom-up project ideation, managing up, and learning to say no to non-promotable work. He also reflects candidly on the trade-offs of accelerated promotion, including increased meetings, reduced flexibility, and a wish that he had stayed at the senior level longer to enjoy better Work-Life Balance, and discusses his brief experience as a Tech Lead Manager and the role of luck versus preparation in career outcomes.

## Key Points

- Ricky went from L3 to L6 at Google in 6 years (L3→L4 in 1.5 years, L4→L5 in 1.5 years, L5→L6 in 3 years), roughly 1.5–2 years faster than average at each step.
- Compensation progression: $180K (L3) → $250K (L4) → $350K (L5) → ~$520K (L6), all roughly average for those levels.
- The key to L3→L4 promotion was Independence — finishing projects without handholding and learning to unblock yourself by asking questions, with a rule of thumb to reach out after ~4 hours of no progress.
- The key to L4→L5 promotion was Scope Ownership — becoming the go-to person for a specific area, finding your own projects with next-level impact, and creating a one-year roadmap with manager buy-in.
- The key to L5→L6 promotion was challenging the status quo within a deeply understood scope, cross-team influence, and established Credibility from a track record of successful projects.
- Bottom-Up Project Ideas were central to his success: he found his own projects rather than waiting for top-down assignments, and ran experiments to identify gaps where he could drive the most impact.
- He explicitly learned to say no to projects that were not at the next level, escalating to his manager when someone else assigned non-promotable work.
- He regrets not staying at the senior level longer, citing the loss of flexibility (midday naps, gym at 3pm) and the increase in meetings at the staff level.
- He briefly became a Tech Lead Manager but wished he had waited longer, noting that management added overhead (performance reviews, calibration, difficult feedback) while the rewarding parts (helping people grow) were already available as a TL.
- He attributes roughly half his career success to luck (stable team, good manager, product area he liked) and half to conscious preparation and effort.
