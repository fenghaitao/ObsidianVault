## Core Summary

Retired Amazon VP Ethan Evans gives a deliberately transparent account of how corporate politics actually works and how to win. His central claim is that politics exists because impact is hard to measure while headcount is easy, so managers are incentivized to empire build, reorgs get loaded with retention and promotion motives, and advancement runs on leverage, relationships, and carefully worded asks rather than technical merit alone. He concludes that the skills are amoral — influence versus manipulation is a question of motive — and that people who hate politics can still protect themselves by choosing a good leader, doing the hardest work, and building leverage.

## Key Points

- Empire building exists because it is rewarded: "counting people is the easiest thing to do." Some companies write down promotion thresholds (Amazon-era whisper number that later got written as ~90 people for director), despite the leadership principle "there's no bonus for additional head count."
- Reorgs trigger on a business change or on personnel moves (cascades when someone quits or is moved), but "there's always something else going on" — retention, setting up promotions, or managing people out by giving them the "garbage can" / window seat.
- "The squeaky wheel gets the grease": being quiet and dutiful can leave you behind because managers focus on the vocal person at risk of leaving. Ask for what you want; there's a middle ground between passive and pushy.
- Polite fictions / careful wording: be "interpersonally warm but professionally firm." Example: "My career is very important to me… I need to understand how important it is to Amazon." Frame requests as help ("How can you help me?") and pre-plan a few key phrases before the room.
- Don't mutiny alone: to escalate a bad manager, gather two or three allies willing to corroborate — a lone complaint reads as "you're the problem." Alternatively, make a blame-free business case to move teams.
- Leverage when being fired: you can save the manager time, control the story you tell the team, and let them feel good about themselves ("it's not their money" — severance). Even so, once you're on a formal PIP you are very likely to be fired.
- Influence vs politics is about motive: the skill sets are identical ("Darth Vader had executive presence in spades"). Assess whether a skilled operator is working for the org, purely for themselves, or somewhere you can negotiate.
- Promotion mechanics: a forward-looking slate/queue of promotions and, at rare levels, quotas (e.g. Google L8-to-L9). Champions/allies must vouch for you; "get your boss on board or get out." Trust is earned in bad times (crises), and likeability ("do people want you in the room?") tips ties.
- Defense tactics: list your unsexy maintenance work (the "skunk trick"), overreact to look thorny, delay ("reorgs cost six months of productivity"), and be the better storyteller since people decide emotionally and then rationalize.
- Understand the other party's self-narrative: "very few humans wake up and say I'm going to be evil today." Managers are either an umbrella (shielding the team) or a funnel (dumping pressure down); change the balance of safety to get them to push back.
- What gets people stuck is "not learning the new skill of the next level — they're doing the current skill harder and harder." Getting advice beats asking for feedback. Growth companies are less political (Amazon grew 100x in his 15 years).
