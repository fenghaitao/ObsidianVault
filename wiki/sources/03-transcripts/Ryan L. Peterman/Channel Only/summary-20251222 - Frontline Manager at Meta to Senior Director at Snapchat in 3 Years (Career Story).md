## Core Summary

The guest (introduced as "Rome" in the outro; the episode intro renders the name as "Ryan") recounts a non-traditional path: a machine-learning PhD at CMU, three years as an IBM Research scientist, then frontline manager at Facebook, a director role at Square, senior director of the camera team at Snapchat, and ultimately CTO of the AI video-generation company HeyGen. His central philosophy is a "north star" — becoming a CTO of an AI company — that anchors every career decision. Along the way he distills the manager-to-director shift from managing people to directing an organization, the Facebook principle that everyone in engineering stays technical, and a radical-honesty approach to hiring.

## Key Points

- Left industrial research after a 5-year PhD plus 3 years at IBM Research because the ~50/50 research/engineering model (IBM Research, Microsoft Research) felt unsustainable; chose software engineering over faculty or quant trading because engineering is a "first class citizen" with broader reach
- Joined fast-growing Facebook around 2009, drawn by its engineering-first culture, fast growth, and potential to influence many people
- Joined Square directly into a director role (team of ~25, grown to 50+ in a year) leading machine learning and data-science infrastructure teams
- The biggest director-level gap was understanding "directing" vs "managing": a director directs an organization toward a better future rather than simply managing people
- In the first half year as director he underperformed and reports questioned whether the layer added value; the turning point was understanding what reports needed (help unblocking and growing to the next level) and building trust first
- Recommends "The Five Dysfunctions of a Team"; its key lesson is that trust is the foundational layer of any successful team
- On job hopping, he never saw it as the only or best path; his framework was a north star goal (CTO of an AI company) and deliberately filling capability gaps (backend, frontend, product) through role choices
- At Snapchat he intentionally chose Director of Camera (the first page of the app) over the more comfortable Director of Data, learning iOS and Android from scratch for ~2 months to inch toward the CTO north star
- Facebook cultural principle: everyone who works on engineering needs to be technical — even VP-level hires did the six-week boot camp writing and reviewing code, which "shocked" him coming from IBM
- He still writes and reviews code (2–3 pull requests a week) as a leader because he cannot speak up confidently without knowing the details
- Time allocation: at the start of each week pick the top three things aligned to the north star and recalibrate; everyone has only 8–12 working hours a day, so top performers allocate time rather than just spend more of it
- Snapchat's culture was surprisingly similar to Facebook/Silicon Valley (move fast, done is better than perfect); LA overall is far more diverse than Silicon Valley, which broadened his view of non-technical users
- Snapchat grew from ~100 people (he was roughly employee #100) to ~3,000 in two years; each time a team doubles it is "like building a new company," requiring adaptive, resilient leadership
- Evan Spiegel has a pixel-level performance bar (comparable to Steve Jobs), values engineers, and reportedly said to never fire the first 15 engineers, who are the company's founding members
- At senior levels he never proactively looked for jobs — opportunities came inbound via personal connections, which he weights heavily so he can weather down-cycles with people he likes
- He anchors decisions to the north star rather than headcount or valuation ("I'm waiting for the time that two people can build a $1B company"); titles and levels belong to the company, not to him
- Hiring philosophy: be radically honest and transparent — tell candidates there is roughly a 1% chance of success and a bumpy road ahead, to sift for the 5%–1% who truly want the journey
- Career advice: use a regret test ("would I regret not doing this in 15 years?"), don't make promotions the objective (they are lagging indicators), control what you can control, and don't always follow conventional wisdom
