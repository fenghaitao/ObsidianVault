## Core Summary

Roberto Ierusalimschy names the languages he thinks every engineer should study in 2026: Haskell (to learn functional programming, with its optional-but-fully-inferred types, and to internalize the correctness-first mindset captured by the old joke that Haskell takes a week to make correct and a year to make efficient), C or an old 8080-style assembler (to understand what the machine actually does), and Scheme (for its economy of ideas). He also recommends looking at very old languages such as SNOBOL — transcribed "Snowball" — because they hold ideas like early pattern matching that today's overly uniform languages have forgotten.

## Key Points

- Haskell is his top pick: it has "everything you need to really learn about functional programming," with types optional everywhere yet still fully inferred.
- The old Haskell-community joke captures the trade-off: in C you spend a week making it efficient and a year making it correct; in Haskell you make it correct in a week and may then spend a year making it efficient.
- C or a very old assembler (e.g., the 8080) teaches what a machine actually does at the basic level; he notes modern assemblers have become too complex for this purpose.
- Scheme is valued for its "economy of ideas" — very few concepts that nonetheless express amazingly powerful programs.
- SNOBOL (transcribed "Snowball") is worth studying as one of the first languages with pattern matching; older languages matter because modern languages have become too uniform (for example, everyone copied C's zero-based indexing).
