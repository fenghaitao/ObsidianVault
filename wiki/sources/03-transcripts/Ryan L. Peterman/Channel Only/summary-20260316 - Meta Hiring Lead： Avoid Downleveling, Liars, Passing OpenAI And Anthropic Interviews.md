## Core Summary

Austin McDonald, a former Meta hiring committee member who led mobile hiring across the company, walks through the behind-the-scenes mechanics of hiring and leveling committees and how candidates get downleveled or upleveled. His central message is that everything in the process is evaluative — the recruiter call, the hiring-manager chat, even the final "do you have questions" — and that senior candidates must anchor every conversation to the scope of their work, because behavioral interviews determine level when coding and system design effectively become pass/fail.

The episode translates the committee's rubric into an actionable playbook: decode the question behind the question, select high-scope stories (the CALR framework as an alternative to STAR), and match each company's cultural value signals — including OpenAI's "feel the AGI" and Anthropic's "hold light and shade."

## Key Points

- Hiring committees intake packets and go to the behavioral interview first to measure a senior candidate's past scope, influence, insight, communication, and organizational level.
- At Meta, initial leveling is set mostly by years of experience and previous title (since Meta levels aren't public), so candidates from title-inflated companies are often downleveled — sometimes from a 50-person manager to a small Meta team.
- Two system design interviews are required before Meta can approve a staff-level hire, and principal/distinguished candidates get additional behavioral interviews and project deep-dives.
- Lying on a resume is "much harder than people expect": seniors run scope checks in the first phone screen, and onsite panels are highly calibrated; follow-up questions and calibrated interviewers catch vague or disconnected stories.
- The single most important story-selection criterion is scope (above relevance, recency, and uniqueness) — you should leave a behavioral interview having told the highest-scope stories that best represent you.
- Meta's five behavioral signal areas are driving results, embracing ambiguity, resolving conflicts, growing continuously, and communicating effectively; recruiters will often share the rubric in advance.
- The core in-interview loop is "decode, select, deliver": figure out which signal the question targets, choose the matching story, and deliver it with an arc; he prefers CALR (context, actions, results, learnings) over STAR.
- Instruction on common senior mistakes: wrong-scope stories, too much irrelevant context, opening yourself to uncharitable interpretations ("the technical debt was your fault"), and neglecting the non-technical (mentoring, organizing, managing up).
- Referrals matter most on the bubble; a VP/director referral who worked with you directly can move a borderline candidate from reject to follow-up, but won't override a unanimous no.
- Compensation: early anchoring is dangerous, and the biggest negotiation mistake is having no competing offers; recruiters owe California/New York candidates band ranges.
- First impressions carry outsized weight — a significant share of decisions is effectively formed in the first 10–15 minutes (social-science estimate ~40%), so candidates should prepare the opening as much as the stories.
- Match company-specific values: OpenAI's "feel the AGI" (optimism about AI's potential) and Anthropic's "hold light and shade" (grasping both positive and negative AI implications).
- His own M2 promotion shows the pattern: running the iOS/Android recruiting pipelines, improving diversity hiring through better interview prep, and clarifying candidate-data quality across the company.
- Advice to his younger self: don't give in to fear — impostor syndrome and fear of rejection kept him from industry internships and slowed his understanding of large organizations.
