## Core Summary

Philip Su, one of the few IC9 (Distinguished Engineer) at Meta, shares his career journey from Microsoft to Meta to OpenAI, covering his rapid promotions, his deliberate demotion from E9 to E7, and his philosophy on career growth. He argues that career advancement boils down to three factors: luck, talent, and hard work, with diminishing returns on long hours but still net-positive output. Su discusses his repeated switches between IC and management tracks, the value of generalists at small companies versus specialists at large ones, and the importance of clear values in making life decisions.

## Key Points

- Career growth is driven by three factors: luck, talent, and hard work -- working longer hours yields diminishing but still positive returns on total output.
- The IC vs Management decision should be treated as a two-way door if you keep your coding skills sharp; many people accidentally walk through a one-way door.
- A great leader is someone the team would elect to lead them; ask "would I want to work for me?"
- At senior levels (E8+), IC and manager skills converge like a diamond shape -- both require vision, communication, and strategic thinking.
- Promotions at the highest levels are qualitative, not checklist-based; it's about whether other E8s believe you are an E8.
- Philip deliberately sought demotion from E9 to E7 when switching back to IC, believing it's better to perform well at a lower level than barely tread water at a higher one.
- To build a strong engineering culture at a remote site, bring a committed landing team of culture carriers who commit to at least two years.
- Mark Zuckerberg and Bosworth both impressed Philip with their deliberate, visible efforts at personal growth and self-improvement.
- John Carmack could drop into a codebase after six months away and provide concrete, actionable technical advice no one else had thought of.
- The market leader has room to experiment and fail; the market follower must match all the leader's checkboxes plus one differentiator -- this is why Philip joined OpenAI rather than any AI competitor.
- Generalists are more valuable at small companies due to quantization effects; specialists become more valuable at large companies.
- Writing well requires reading well and being willing to rewrite multiple times; Philip initially disrespected soft skills but later recognized their value.
- Philip advises his younger self to spend more time clarifying personal values before pursuing goals, and to be careful about "catching the car" -- achieving a goal with no plan for what comes next.
