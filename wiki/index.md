## Concepts

*(Frameworks, methodologies, theories — TitleCase filenames)*

- [[100% Automation Threshold]] — [[Cat Wu]]'s principle that an automation which works 90-95% of the time isn't really an automation at all — you still have to check its work, which defeats the purpose — so it's worth the extra effort to close the gap to fully reliable.
- [[10x Learner Vs 10x Doer]] — [[Zevi Arnovitz]]'s reframe, from his Failure Corner story at [[Wix]], that a junior person's job is to be the best learner in the room, not to impress with independent output — since senior colleagues expect growth, not immediate mastery.
- [[4x4 Debugging Framework]] — [[Lazar]]'s named framework ("4x4," by analogy to a 4-wheel-drive vehicle getting you out of the mud) for unblocking an AI coding agent when it gets stuck on a bug — four escalating tools, each tried once before moving to the next.
- [[AB Testing Skepticism]] — [[Jason Cohen]]'s contrarian take: most A/B testing, as practiced by most companies, doesn't actually work — both because it can't test the things that matter most (strategy, vision) and because most measured "wins" on smaller details are statistical false positives.
- [[Abundance Mindset]] — [[Sam Lesson]]'s term for the mindset underlying good etiquette: treating any given social/business interaction as one of many future opportunities rather than a single, scarce, must-not-fail moment — the mental posture that produces a [[Low Heart Rate]].
- [[Accumulating Advantages]] — [[Keith Rabois]]'s core due-diligence question for early-stage investing (asked for ~25 years): does this business have a path to advantages that compound over time, making it better and easier to win as it grows — not just an initial edge?
- [[Active Listening Three Levels]] — [[Rachel Lockett]]'s three-level listening model (internal, focused, global) distinguishing surface-level listening from hearing what's beneath someone's words.
- [[Active Social Network]] — Proprietary metric ("ASN") built at [[Zynga]] measuring how many reciprocal social interactions ("round trips") a player had with a friend or another player — e.g., taking a turn and having it returned, gifting and being gifted back.
- [[Actuators]] — Motorized components that produce movement in robots; cited by [[Caitlin Kalinowski]] as one of the key supply-chain bottlenecks (alongside magnets, silicon, and RAM) gating at-scale [[Humanoid Robots]] production.
- [[Adjacent User Theory]] — [[Bangaly Kaba]]'s growth framework, cited by [[Elena Verna]]: the cheapest, highest-opportunity growth lever is converting "adjacent users" (aware of, maybe tried, the product but not retained) rather than chasing an entirely new market segment.
- [[Agency]] — Agency, as framed by [[Max Schoening]] (Head of Product, [[Notion]]), is the disposition to believe the world around you is malleable and act on that belief — to just make things rather than waiting for permission or citing a "skill issue" as a reason something can't be done.
- [[Agency Control Trade-off]] — [[Aishwarya Naresh Reganti]] and [[Kiriti Badam]]'s term for the second of two fundamental ways AI products differ from traditional software (the first being [[Non-Determinism In AI Products|non-determinism]]): every time a product hands decision-making capability ("agency") to an agentic system, the builder relinquishes a corresponding amount of human control. Because the agent's reliability can't be verified up front, agency should be extended only in proportion to demonstrated trust.
- [[Agent Training Loop]] — [[Jason Lemkin]]'s repeatable process for making an AI go-to-market agent work: ingest your best material, train the agent by answering its clarifying questions, then spend roughly an hour or two a day for ~30 days correcting its live mistakes.
- [[Agentic Engineering]] — [[Simon Willison]]'s term for the professional discipline of using coding agents (which write, run, debug, and test code themselves) to build production-ready software — distinct from casual "[[Vibe Coding]]," where the user doesn't look at, review, or understand the generated code.
- [[AGI Pilled]] — [[Cat Wu]]'s term for the hardest calibration problem in AI product management: figuring out the "right amount" of designing for a hypothetical, fully-capable future model versus designing for what the current model can actually do well.
- [[AI Content Labeling]] — [[Adam Mosseri]]'s (Head of [[Instagram]]) stated policy stance that AI-generated content should not be filtered or judged based on the tool used to create it, but that users should be told whether content and/or accounts are AI-generated or human, so they can make their own informed decisions.
- [[AI IQ Scaling]] — [[Marc Andreessen]]'s argument that human intelligence is capped by biology (~110-160 IQ, with 160 being Einstein/Feynman-level), while AI has no equivalent ceiling and will likely scale well past it (180, 200, 250+), making "human-equivalent" AGI a transient milestone rather than an endpoint.
- [[AI Job Apocalypse]] — The narrative — pushed back on by [[Dan Shipper]] — that AI will directly and broadly eliminate large swaths of jobs, which he argues is overblown alongside the related "[[SaaS Apocalypse]]" narrative.
- [[AI Product Success Triangle]] — [[Kiriti Badam]]'s three-dimensional framework for what distinguishes companies that build AI products successfully: "great leaders, good culture, and technical progress." The framing insists "it's never always technical — every technology problem is a people problem first."
- [[AI Safety Three Layers]] — [[Boris Cherny]]'s three-layer framework for how [[Anthropic]] studies AI safety, in increasing order of realism: alignment/mechanistic interpretability, evals, and real-world deployment behavior.
- [[Allocation Economy]] — "The allocation economy" is an essay/concept by [[Dan Shipper]] arguing that as AI takes over more execution, human work with AI increasingly resembles being a manager — allocating, checking in on, and correcting AI "reports" — rather than doing the underlying task directly.
- [[Alpha Beta Hiring And Process Framework]] — [[Matt MacInnis]]'s finance-derived framework for calibrating hiring and process to context: "alpha" (high-upside, volatile) people/processes suit 0-to-1 work, "low-beta" (reliable, predictable) ones suit mature must-not-fail systems, and process exists solely to suppress beta at the cost of suppressing alpha.
- [[Anti-AI Sentiment]] — [[Benedict Evans]]'s framing of the public backlash against AI as a "big fuzzy mess" that mixes legitimate grievances with objectively false claims, rather than a single coherent position.
- [[Automation Is A Lie]] — "Automation is a lie" is [[Dan Shipper]]'s recurring central claim in this episode: every deployed AI agent still requires an attentive, invested human to keep it useful, so increased automation does not translate into proportionally less human work.
- [[Automation Vs Augmentation]] — The "automation versus augmentation fork," as named by [[Max Schoening]] (Head of Product, [[Notion]]), is the open question of whether faster, near-instant AI inference will push interaction back toward direct manipulation (augmentation — a human still molds the work in real time) or toward fully automated, queued/delegated agent work (automation — a human hands off a task and reviews later).
- [[Bad Vs Sad Framework]] — The "bad vs. sad" quality framework, used by [[Fiona Fung]]'s teams at [[Anthropic]], lets each team define its own threshold for distinguishing irrecoverable ("bad") failures from recoverable ("sad") ones.
- [[Ballmer Peak]] — Concept (originating from an XKCD comic, named for Steve Ballmer) describing a narrow window of alcohol consumption at which a person is at peak insight/creativity, before performance collapses past that point.
- [[Barrels And Ammunition]] — [[Keith Rabois]]'s framework: within any organization, only a small number of people ("barrels") can independently drive an initiative from inception to success; everyone else is "ammunition" that barrels can deploy.
- [[Behavior Vs Identity]] — Core principle behind [[Dr. Becky Kennedy]]'s "good inside" idea — that a person's identity (they are fundamentally good) must be treated as separate from their behavior (which may be problematic), because collapsing the two together is what makes difficult conversations unproductive and defensive.
- [[Best Idea Wins Culture]] — [[Qasar Younis]]'s description of [[Applied Intuition]]'s emotion-minimized decision culture, where the best idea wins regardless of who proposed it.
- [[Binding Receptor Theory of Product-Market Fit]] — [[Matt MacInnis]]'s drug-discovery analogy for product-market fit: the market either has or lacks the "binding receptors" for a product, a fact marketing can raise awareness of but never change.
- [[Bitter Lesson]] — Principle from [[Rich Sutton]]'s essay "The Bitter Lesson" (~2019): more general AI models/methods reliably outperform more specific, hand-engineered ones over the long run — cited by [[Boris Cherny]] as required reading for the [[Claude Code]] team and a core design philosophy.
- [[Bloom Two Sigma Effect]] — Research finding (cited by [[Marc Andreessen]]) that one-on-one tutoring is the single most reliable method known for improving educational outcomes, routinely raising a student's performance by two standard deviations — from the 50th percentile to roughly the 99th.
- [[Bob The Monster]] — [[Molly Graham]]'s technique for handling the emotional turbulence of organizational change: externalize anger/fear/impostor-syndrome reactions into a named, personified "monster" whose sole job is to make you the worst version of yourself, so you can notice its voice without obeying it.
- [[Bold Beat]] — [[Mark Pincus]]'s term (developed with [[Bing Gordon]] at [[Zynga]]) for taking something that has become boring or rote (like poker) and adding "a whole new dimension" to it — most often a social dimension — to reinvigorate it.
- [[Bottom-Up AI Adoption]] — [[Sherwin Wu]]'s observation that successful enterprise AI deployments require both executive-level buy-in *and* genuine bottom-up adoption from actual employees — top-down mandates alone (without employees who are excited, evangelizing, and building best practices organically) tend to produce negative-ROI deployments.
- [[Boundaries Vs Requests]] — [[Dr. Becky Kennedy]]'s distinction between a true boundary — "what you tell someone else you will do," requiring nothing of the other person — and a request, which depends on the other person's compliance and therefore isn't a boundary at all, however it's phrased.
- [[Build For The Model 6 Months Out]] — [[Boris Cherny]]'s product-timing philosophy: design and build for the AI model's capability roughly 6 months in the future, not its current capability — accepting weak product-market fit at first so the product is ready to "click" the moment the more capable model arrives.
- [[Build In Public]] — "Building in public" is the practice of openly sharing your work, failures, and knowledge (via YouTube, LinkedIn, courses, etc.) as a career strategy — cited by [[Lazar]] as literally how his [[Professional Vibe Coder]] job came to exist, before any company had formally hired him for it.
- [[Builders Vs Information Movers]] — [[Nikhyl Singhal]]'s central dichotomy for the current product-management job market: "builders" (people who love hands-on making, prototyping, shipping) are thriving in the AI era, while "information movers" (whose core skill was relaying/translating information up and down an org hierarchy) are being obsoleted — roughly half of existing PMs fall into each camp, per Singhal.
- [[Building Blocks Progression]] — [[Cat Wu]]'s framing of [[Claude Code]] and [[Claude Cowork]]'s product roadmap as a sequence of building blocks: single-task reliability, then multiple simultaneous tasks, then many (50-100+) agents running in parallel and remotely.
- [[Business Process Automation Opportunity]] — [[Sherwin Wu]]'s bullish, underrated-opportunity thesis: most of the world's economically significant work is repeatable, high-determinism "business process" work (support scripts, standard operating procedures, back-office operations), not the open-ended, non-repeatable knowledge work (like software engineering) that Silicon Valley focuses on and that tools like Codex are built for.
- [[CAD]] — Computer-aided design — the dense, solid engineering geometry work (understanding friction, weight, contact) that [[Caitlin Kalinowski]] says current AI (LLMs, video models) cannot yet do well.
- [[CAMEL Pattern]] — Architectural approach to mitigating "[[Prompt Injection]]"/"[[Lethal Trifecta]]" risk, proposed in a Google DeepMind paper and cited by [[Simon Willison]] as the most credible partial mitigation he's seen: split an AI agent into a privileged agent (which the trusted user talks to and which can take meaningful actions) and a quarantined agent (which is exposed to untrusted/malicious input but cannot itself take consequential actions).
- [[CASE (Claude Accelerates Sustainable Hypergrowth)]] — Internal [[Anthropic]] growth-platform initiative (acknowledged by [[Amol]] as an awkward acronym) to automate growth experimentation itself using [[Claude]] — not just building growth features faster, but having Claude identify, build, test, and analyze growth experiments with progressively less human involvement.
- [[Centaur And Reverse Centaur]] — A metaphor (credited by [[Lenny Rachitsky]] to writer [[Cory Doctorow]]) for two opposite modes of human/AI collaboration: a "centaur" (human body, horse body below — human in charge of the tool) versus a "reverse centaur" (horse head, human body — the AI/machine in charge, with the human just doing its bidding).
- [[Chatter Driven Development]] — [[Alexander Imbiricos]]'s half-joking coinage for a looser alternative to spec-driven development: an agent monitors team communication channels and ships small fixes reactively without a formal spec.
- [[Chief Orchestration Officer]] — [[Jason Lemkin]]'s term for the internal role required to run a fleet of AI go-to-market agents day-to-day, exemplified by [[Amelia]] — distinct from the vendor-side Forward Deployed Engineer role.
- [[China As State Extension]] — [[Qasar Younis]]'s argument that Chinese tech giants like [[Huawei]] should be understood as extensions of the state rather than ordinary profit-seeking companies.
- [[Close To The Metal]] — [[Mark Pincus]]'s principle that the best product CEOs/founders stay personally involved in pixel-level, minute product details rather than delegating the most important UX/product decisions to their least experienced people.
- [[Co-Founder Relationship Practices]] — [[Rachel Lockett]]'s set of practices (vows, quarterly check-ins, personality-typing tools) for maintaining a healthy co-founder relationship, which she says causes ~65% of startup failures when it breaks down.
- [[Cocktail Party Distribution]] — [[Mark Pincus]]'s metaphor for consumer/social distribution: a "great cocktail party" is a place people are excited (not just obligated) to be, because they get real value there — historically, "leads" (connections, dates, jobs, listings).
- [[Cognitive Surrender]] — Tony Fadell's term for the failure mode of trusting AI tools to make product, architecture, or design decisions end-to-end without human oversight.
- [[Compounding Plus Power Law Plus Entropy]] — [[Matt MacInnis]]'s three-part systems framework: outcomes follow power-law distributions, organizations naturally decay toward entropy/disorder, and sustained executive-injected energy/intensity is the only real antidote to that decay.
- [[Comprehension Vs Friction]] — [[Stewart Butterfield]]'s prioritization framework: whether a user understands what a product does and why matters more than reducing the number of clicks or steps to use it, per *[[Don't Make Me Think]]*.
- [[Compressing The Talent Stack]] — Framework credited by [[Alexander Imbiricos]] to Scott Belsky: AI lets individuals do more across traditionally separate disciplines, removing communication-boundary overhead between roles.
- [[Computer Errands]] — "Computer errands," a term coined by Every COO [[Brandon Gall]], describes the emerging territory of using personal AI agents for everyday personal tasks (e.g., ordering groceries) rather than work tasks.
- [[Connecting Before Correcting]] — [[Dr. Becky Kennedy]]'s principle that people cooperate once they feel seen as a full person embedded in their own priorities — not as an object to be maneuvered toward what you want — so connection has to precede any request or correction.
- [[Contextual Actions]] — [[Alexander Imbiricos]]'s video-game-inspired UX concept: an agent should proactively surface a specific, relevant action at the moment it's useful, rather than requiring explicit invocation or sending generic notifications — the design rationale behind [[Atlas]].
- [[Continuous Calibration Continuous Development]] — "Continuous Calibration, Continuous Development" (CCCD) is [[Aishwarya Naresh Reganti]] and [[Kiriti Badam]]'s named life cycle for building AI products, deliberately named after CI/CD (continuous integration/continuous deployment) to signal that AI product development is an iterative loop rather than a linear ship-and-move-on process. It pairs a "continuous development" side (scope capability, curate data, design evaluation metrics, deploy) with a "continuous calibration" side (observe real user behavior, spot emerging error patterns, fix issues, and design new evaluation metrics for patterns you didn't originally anticipate).
- [[Conway's Law]] — Any organization that designs a system produces a design mirroring its own communication structure; invoked by [[Matt MacInnis]] describing an inherited product org as "locally optimized but globally incoherent."
- [[Criticize In Public]] — Management philosophy [[Keith Rabois]] absorbed from a founder he worked with: deliver negative feedback about a person's performance in front of colleagues rather than privately, on the theory that private feedback optimizes for the individual while public feedback optimizes for the whole system.
- [[Crossing the Chasm]] — The classic technology-adoption-curve model (early adopters, later adopters, laggards); Tony Fadell says he updated/adapted this graphic in *Build* to reflect how his teams tailor marketing language to each adopter segment across product versions.
- [[Crossing The Threshold]] — [[Nikhyl Singhal]]'s single most important piece of career advice for the current moment: finding the courage to actively reinvent how you work, despite the exhaustion, ego cost, and identity disruption that reinvention requires.
- [[CTO Persona Pattern]] — [[Zevi Arnovitz]]'s technique of prompting an AI project/agent to behave as a specific, opinionated human role — originally a "CTO" — rather than a generic assistant, explicitly countering default chatbot sycophancy so it can push back on bad decisions instead of agreeing with them.
- [[Culture Bank]] — [[Eric Ries]]'s framework (the "Todd Park rule," which [[Todd Park]] learned from [[Howard Schultz]]) for how mission-aligned behavior is trained into employees as ordinary deposits into a shared "culture bank," rather than treated as exceptional individual heroism.
- [[Customer Love Sprint]] — A two-week initiative [[Jessica Fain]] ran on [[Slack]]'s core product team (after taking it over from her chief-of-staff role): the entire engineering team paused other work to fix small product rough edges/polish issues, with engineers choosing what to work on, the only rule being that each person had to ship something good for users.
- [[Dark Factory Pattern]] — [[Simon Willison]]'s term (also "software factory") for a stage of AI-assisted software development beyond "[[Agentic Engineering]]": nobody writes the code, and nobody reads the code either — yet professional quality practices and expectations still apply, unlike careless "vibe coding." Named by analogy to factory automation so complete that the factory floor can run with the lights off, since no humans need to be physically present.
- [[Day 365 Retention]] — Metric tracking what fraction of users are still active exactly one year after acquisition; per [[Mark Pincus]], [[Zynga]] was reportedly the only consumer company at the time tracking it, and he claims (statistically) that the most valuable companies in the world tend to have the highest Day 365 retention.
- [[Deeply Feeling Kids]] — [[Dr. Becky Kennedy]]'s name for a temperament pattern — in kids and, by extension, adults — whose core fear is being "too much," often labeled dramatic or oppositional, who push away the very people they need most in the moments they need them, thereby confirming their own fear.
- [[Deliberate Understaffing]] — [[Matt MacInnis]]'s ([[Rippling]]) practice of always staffing projects below their ideal level on purpose, since overstaffing causes politics and lower-priority "crust" while calibrated understaffing is the lesser evil.
- [[Demo Don't Memo]] — "Demo don't memo" was [[Lovable]]'s internal company motto for 2025, per [[Lazar]]: instead of writing documents, holding meetings, and trying to convey a product vision in words, build a working prototype (often in Lovable itself) in minutes to hours and hand it over directly.
- [[Demographic Collapse Necessitates AI]] — [[Marc Andreessen]]'s central macro thesis: AI is arriving "miraculously" well-timed to offset two converging structural problems — 50 years of surprisingly low productivity/technological growth, and accelerating global demographic collapse (below-replacement birth rates, including in China).
- [[Design As Bottleneck]] — [[Evan Spiegel]]'s deliberate philosophy at [[Snapchat]]: design approval is an intentional gate that everything must pass before shipping, even though this slows down the shipping process — because it's what produces a cohesive customer experience.
- [[Design Hiring Archetypes]] — [[Jenny Wen]]'s three preferred designer hiring profiles for the current AI-accelerated era: strong generalists ("block-shaped"), deep T-shaped specialists, and "craft new grads."
- [[Design Process]] — The traditional design process — research, ideation, divergence, convergence, prototyping, and implementation, historically justified by the assumption that implementation is expensive — is discussed by [[Andrew Ambrosino]] as partially obsolete, but not dead in the way it is sometimes claimed to be.
- [[Determinate Vs Indeterminate Optimism]] — [[Peter Thiel]]'s 2x2 framework (optimism/pessimism × determinate/indeterminate), applied by [[Marc Andreessen]] to explain Silicon Valley and his own firm's venture strategy: a "determinate optimist" has a specific plan for how the future gets better (Thiel's example: Elon Musk — electric cars, then solar, then Mars); an "indeterminate optimist" believes the future will be better without being able to specify exactly how or why.
- [[Digital Nutrition Regulation]] — Tony Fadell's analogy calling for platforms and product designers to regulate/label digital consumption the way physical food is regulated and labeled, to counter addictive design in phones, social media, and AI chatbots.
- [[Direct In Public Trust In Private]] — [[Jeetu Patel]]'s explicit rejection of the standard management advice "praise in public, criticize in private": be direct and critique openly in public, while reserving private moments specifically to build and reinforce trust.
- [[Director's Oath]] — One of the named governance frameworks [[Eric Ries]] formalizes in *[[Incorruptible (book)|Incorruptible]]* — a formal commitment board directors make to uphold a company's mission, distinct from and in addition to standard fiduciary duty under [[Shareholder Primacy]].
- [[Distribution Moat]] — [[Benedict Evans]]'s argument that as AI chatbot products commoditize and converge on similar quality, existing distribution (not product superiority) becomes the deciding factor in adoption — a bigger moat than the underlying model itself.
- [[Divine Discontent]] — Jeff Bezos's idea, cited by [[Stewart Butterfield]]: customer expectations rise faster than any single company can satisfy them, driven by the best experience a customer has had anywhere, not just within one product category.
- [[Do Not Promise What You Cannot Control]] — [[Molly Graham]]'s rule for leaders: never promise outcomes actually outside your control (stability, specific titles, "you'll never be laid off"), since a broken promise demoralizes people faster than almost anything else.
- [[Dogfooding]] — Dogfooding — using your own unfinished product internally to build and improve it — is described by [[Andrew Ambrosino]] as central to how [[OpenAI]]'s [[Codex]] app was and continues to be developed.
- [[Don't Box The Model In]] — [[Boris Cherny]]'s core [[Claude Code]] design philosophy: instead of constraining a model to a narrow component of a larger orchestrated system (fixed step-by-step workflows, curated context, an "orchestrator" directing it), give the model tools, a goal, and minimal scaffolding, and let it decide which tools to use and in what order.
- [[Elephant Curve]] — [[Jason Cohen]]'s term (named for the shape it traces — a rising "trunk" followed by a sagging "butt") for how marketing/acquisition channels actually behave over time: not a simple S-curve that plateaus, but one that eventually declines as the channel saturates and audiences become fatigued or move on.
- [[Enthusiastic Rehire Test]] — [[Stripe]]'s talent-evaluation question, per [[Rachel Lockett]]: "would you enthusiastically rehire this person for the same role?" — a binary forcing function for talent clarity, not a mandate to fire.
- [[Escalation Is A Tool]] — A rule [[Molly Graham]] attributes to [[Mark Zuckerberg]]: when two people of roughly equal power reach a genuine impasse, escalating together to a shared manager is not "tattling" but the correct, fastest tool for getting unblocked.
- [[Escalations Are A Gift]] — [[Matt MacInnis]]'s stance that withholding feedback or an escalation to avoid inconveniencing someone is selfish, not considerate; [[Rippling]] runs a dedicated escalations team trained to trace issues to true root causes.
- [[Ethos And Integrity]] — [[Eric Ries]]'s proposed antidote to [[Financial Gravity]] in *[[Incorruptible (book)|Incorruptible]]*: a company resists decay by combining "ethos" (a clear, written purpose) with "integrity" (structural governance mechanisms that enforce it), not by relying on good intentions alone.
- [[Evals As Product Definition]] — [[Cat Wu]]'s practice of using a small number of well-built evals — as few as ten — to concretely define what "success" means for an ambiguous AI feature, rather than relying only on prose goals or PRDs.
- [[Evals Vs Production Monitoring]] — [[Kiriti Badam]]'s rejection of the "false dichotomy" that either evals or production monitoring alone can solve AI product reliability — his argument that the two are complementary tools catching different classes of problems, and that "this notion [that] only one of them is going to solve things for you... is completely dismissible."
- [[Everyone In Market At Once]] — [[Jason Lemkin]]'s description of 2026's go-to-market demand condition: whereas historically only ~3-5% of prospects in a category were actively "in market," AI-category adoption has pushed that figure above 50% in many categories.
- [[Expert Witness Syndrome]] — [[Mark Pincus]]'s term for the position of individual contributors early in their careers: closest to the primary data and probably closest to the right answer, yet furthest from the authority to make the decision — "called to the stand," giving testimony, then excused while others decide and they must live with the consequences.
- [[Exploration Based Ranking]] — A recommendation-systems distinction described by [[Adam Mosseri]] (Head of [[Instagram]]) between exploitation-based ranking (showing content already known to perform well, based on existing engagement data) and exploration-based ranking (deliberately testing content a user might like even though the system doesn't yet have strong evidence for it).
- [[Exponential Thinking]] — [[Anthropic]]'s core internal mental model, described by [[Amol]]: because underlying model capability grows exponentially, the product value an AI company can deliver compounds exponentially too — very unlike a typical non-AI product, where added features raise user value only incrementally (Amol estimates 30-50% more value over two years for a normal product, versus 100-1000x for an AI-native one).
- [[Exposure Therapy Onboarding]] — [[Zevi Arnovitz]]'s recommended gradual path for non-technical people intimidated by code to become AI-assisted builders, likened explicitly to exposure therapy: start in pure conversation, and only slowly work up to looking directly at code.
- [[Exposure Time]] — "Exposure time" (or "exposure hours") is the practice — described by [[Lazar]] and attributed by him to an earlier [[Lenny's Podcast]] guest — of deliberately spending time exposed to great work (designs, products, onboarding flows, people) as the primary way to build [[Taste]] and judgment, as distinct from spending that time building or coding.
- [[Extraordinary Results Require Extraordinary Effort]] — Framework (credited to Dan Gil of Carvana, used by [[Matt MacInnis]]) holding that extraordinary effort is necessary but not sufficient for an extraordinary outcome — and that comfort at work is itself a sign something has gone wrong.
- [[Feelings Overpower Skills]] — [[Dr. Becky Kennedy]]'s core explanation for bad behavior at any age: "kids are born with all the feelings and none of the skills to manage feelings" — behavior is a manifestation of a gap between the feeling someone is having and the skill they have available to manage it, not a moral failing or a deliberate choice.
- [[Fifty Percent Growth Rule]] — [[Molly Graham]]'s rule of thumb for sustainable headcount growth: ~50% annual growth is the happiest rate; 100% (doubling) is "manageable"; anything beyond doubling in a year is "signing yourself up for a world of pain."
- [[Figure It Out Principle]] — Leadership response coined by [[Eric Ries]] from a [[Cloudflare]] story: when a junior employee raises an idea that conflicts with short-term financial interest, a mission-driven leader says "let's figure it out" instead of shutting the idea down.
- [[Financial Gravity]] — [[Eric Ries]]'s central antagonist concept in *[[Incorruptible (book)|Incorruptible]]*: the internal force that drags successful organizations toward mediocrity and extraction once they succeed, not competition from outside.
- [[Forward Deployed Engineer]] — A person (or small team) whose job is to set up and continuously maintain a company's shared AI agent — a role [[Dan Shipper]] says emerged from "every agent needs a human."
- [[Founder Personality Defines Culture]] — [[Molly Graham]]'s culture thesis: roughly 80% of a company's culture is defined by the personality of its founder(s), not declared values or mission statements — an operator's job is to extend that culture, not invent a separate one.
- [[Founder Reset Heuristic]] — [[Qasar Younis]]'s advice that founders roughly two years in without informative market signal should consider a hard reset of the company's foundation rather than iterating on the surface.
- [[Freedom Through Constraints]] — [[Amol]]'s life and business principle: constraints, rather than purely limiting you, can create freedom by eliminating excess choice — once a path is clearly the only viable one, decision-making becomes simpler and more focused.
- [[Full-Stack Builder Model]] — [[Tomer Cohen]]'s (LinkedIn CPO) restructuring of product development: individual "full-stack builders" from any function take an idea from research through launch themselves in small cross-functional pods, keeping five human traits (vision, empathy, communication, creativity, judgment) while custom internal AI agents automate the rest.
- [[Give Away Your Legos]] — [[Molly Graham]]'s signature career/management framework: as a company scales, treat whatever you've mastered as a pile of Legos you must repeatedly hand off so you can move on to a bigger, less-defined pile, rather than clinging to the scope you've mastered.
- [[Give To Get]] — [[Sam Lesson]]'s principle that good conversation is a reciprocal exchange, not a one-sided extraction or monologue — modeled explicitly as a game of ping-pong, where each party alternates offering something and responding.
- [[Go-To-Market Engineer]] — [[Jeanne DeWitt Grosser]]'s emerging technical role: builds custom internal AI agents that replicate and exceed the best practices of a company's top-performing salespeople on specific GTM workflows.
- [[Good Friction]] — [[Amol]]'s activation/onboarding philosophy: deliberately-added friction — specifically, questions that help a product understand who a user is and recommend the right features to them — reliably beats a naively frictionless "get them into the product as fast as possible" design, contrary to conventional UX wisdom.
- [[GROW Model]] — [[Rachel Lockett]]'s four-category coaching-question framework (Goal, Reality, Options, Way forward) for helping a direct report solve their own problem instead of being given an answer.
- [[Growth Mindset]] — Growth mindset, as described by [[Fiona Fung]], is the disposition to keep learning and to let go of approaches that made you successful in the past when they no longer fit a changed landscape — cited as her primary explanation for why some engineers thrive amid rapid AI-driven change while others become frustrated or fearful.
- [[Growth Stall Diagnostic Framework]] — [[Jason Cohen]]'s sequential five-question framework for diagnosing why a product/company's growth has stalled — and, equally, for finding new growth — applied strictly in order, since fixing a downstream issue doesn't help if an upstream one remains unaddressed.
- [[GTM As Product]] — [[Jeanne DeWitt Grosser]]'s framework: treat the full customer journey (awareness through multi-year retention) as a designed product, backed by multi-dimensional segmentation, since as products commoditize the buying experience itself becomes the differentiator.
- [[Guardrails Do Not Work]] — [[Sander Schulhoff]]'s central thesis: AI guardrails don't meaningfully defend against prompt injection/jailbreaking, since the attack space against any LLM is effectively infinite and adaptive human attackers break state-of-the-art guardrails in 10-30 attempts.
- [[Harder Is Easier]] — [[Eric Ries]]'s principle in *[[Incorruptible (book)|Incorruptible]]* that the harder, more expensive-looking short-term choice is often the one that compounds into durable long-term value — resisting [[Financial Gravity]] pays off, even though it looks costlier in the moment.
- [[Hardware Compile Cycle]] — The long feedback loop of hardware development — where late-stage changes (unlike a quick software recompile) require rebuilding physical tooling, prototypes, and validation cycles — illustrated by [[Quest 2]]'s camera-count reduction causing a costly late-stage spec mismatch.
- [[High Agency High Accountability]] — High agency, high accountability is a paired team value described by [[Fiona Fung]] as core to how [[Anthropic]]'s [[Claude Code]] and [[Claude Cowork]] teams operate: everyone is expected to take initiative on problems (high agency), balanced by clear ownership of the outcome and the underlying hypothesis being tested (high accountability).
- [[Hive Mind]] — [[Jason Lemkin]]'s term, inspired by *Pluribus*, for a future state of AI go-to-market agents sharing data and learnings across all of a company's customers/agents simultaneously, unlike today's siloed per-segment agents.
- [[Hoarding Things You Know How To Do]] — [[Simon Willison]]'s long-standing career-advice principle, newly supercharged by AI: build value by accumulating a large personal backlog of small techniques, tools, and verified experiments you've tried, so that when a new problem appears you can recognize and combine relevant prior solutions others wouldn't think to connect.
- [[Home Field Advantage]] — [[Jacob Warwick]]'s negotiation tactic: deliberately choose the meeting environment and time that puts you at your best, since more deals close outside a formal boardroom setting (a walk, a coffee, a lunch, a golf outing) than inside one, and a comfortable, non-adversarial setting produces better outcomes for both sides.
- [[Human Typing Speed As Bottleneck]] — [[Alexander Imbiricos]]'s central AGI-timeline argument: the limiting factor on AI's economic impact is not model capability or compute, but literal human typing/reviewing speed, since humans still have to prompt and validate agent output.
- [[Humanoid Robots]] — Human-shaped robots discussed by [[Caitlin Kalinowski]] as the most visible manifestation of [[Physical AI]] — currently advanced prototypes, not yet ready for at-scale (hundreds of thousands to millions of units) deployment.
- [[Hyperrealistic Work-Like Activities]] — [[Stewart Butterfield]]'s term for meetings, decks, and analysis structurally indistinguishable from genuine work but producing no meaningful value, arising when headcount outpaces "known valuable work to do"; see [[Parkinson's Law (Organizational)]].
- [[I Believe You And I Believe In You]] — [[Dr. Becky Kennedy]]'s two-part support formula for helping someone (kid or adult) through a hard task or feeling: validate that the difficulty is real ("I believe you") while also holding a more capable vision of them than they can currently see for themselves ("I believe in you").
- [[Incremental Correctness]] — Incremental correctness is a core value [[Max Schoening]] (Head of Product, [[Notion]]) says he holds personally and tries to instill at Notion: get really, really good at *iterating* toward quality, rather than trying to isolate yourself until a product is fully finished ("[[Obviously Good]]") before shipping anything.
- [[Invisible Leader]] — Concept coined circa 1920 by management theorist [[Mary Parker Follett]], presented by [[Eric Ries]] as the intellectual origin of *[[Incorruptible (book)|Incorruptible]]*'s closing argument: a company's true leader is not its formal head but the shared sense of common purpose that head is skilled at instilling in the people who work there.
- [[J Curve Versus Stairs]] — [[Molly Graham]]'s career-growth framework (drawn originally by [[Chamath Palihapitiya]]): most careers are predictable "stairs" (incremental promotions), but the more valuable path is a "J-curve" — jumping into a role you're underqualified for, falling for ~6-9 months, then climbing to a level stairs could never reach.
- [[Jevons Paradox]] — Classical economic paradox — invoked by [[Benedict Evans]] (misheard as "jeans paradox" in the transcript) — describing how making something more efficient/cheaper can increase, rather than decrease, total consumption of it, and by extension the surrounding demand for labor.

- [[SPOTAK Hiring Framework]] — [[Matt MacInnis]]'s candidate-evaluation checklist (smart, passionate, optimistic, tenacious, adaptable, kind) used to decode hiring intuition into articulable reasons.
- [[Storytelling]] — Tony Fadell's framing of storytelling as core product-building craft, not a marketing afterthought: the practice of communicating a product's "why," not just its "what," so it becomes meaningful and relatable to customers.
- [[Strategy Should Hurt]] — Phrase coined by [[Claire Hughes Johnson]] and adopted by [[Molly Graham]] as rule #4 of her "[[Six Rules For Goals And Alignment]]": if a strategy or goal-setting process isn't painful to make, it isn't actually prioritizing.
- [[Strobe Light Calendar Model]] — [[Jessica Fain]]'s mental model for how executives actually experience their day: like a strobe light going off, jumping from a finance budget meeting to an executive interview to a people problem to a legal problem to a product review, with no time between to "center" any one person's issue — meaning by the time you meet them, they haven't thought about your topic since your last conversation, even though you've been preparing for weeks.
- [[Stuart Plus Two More]] — Design-review norm at [[Slack]], described by [[Jessica Fain]]: after [[Stewart Butterfield]] gave explicit design direction in a review ("I want the button here, the interaction to be this"), the team would build exactly what he asked for *plus* two additional alternative versions they felt good about, creating a structured forum to debate the merits of each approach.
- [[Sturdy Leadership]] — [[Dr. Becky Kennedy]]'s concept of "sturdiness" as the essence of good leadership (in parenting and at work): the ability to see someone else's emotional experience as real for them, while not being overwhelmed by it — holding on to your own judgment and decision regardless.
- [[Success Disasters]] — [[Amol]]'s term for the ~70% of his time as [[Anthropic]]'s head of growth spent not on proactive optimization, but on firefighting problems caused by things going *too* well — acquisition, activation, and monetization systems all breaking under extreme, rapid scale.
- [[Supervisor-Subagent Pattern]] — [[Aishwarya Naresh Reganti]]'s account of the multi-agent architecture that actually works in practice: a single supervisor agent orchestrating narrower sub-agents that execute delegated work — contrasted with the "misunderstood" (not overhyped, but widely mis-modeled) belief that peer-to-peer, function-divided agents can simply be wired together into an "agent utopia."
- [[Surgical Team Model]] — [[Sherwin Wu]]'s engineering-management philosophy, borrowed from a prediction in *The Mythical Man-Month* (1970s): treat top-performing engineers like a lead surgeon in an operating room, with everyone else (including the manager) as support staff who anticipate needs and hand over the right tool at the right moment.
- [[Task Vs Job]] — [[Benedict Evans]]'s central analytic framework for thinking about AI and jobs: a "job" is a bundle of "tasks," and automating one task inside a job doesn't necessarily remove the reason the job (or the person doing it) exists.
- [[Taste]] — "Taste," as discussed by [[Andrew Ambrosino]] of [[OpenAI]], is the curatorial judgment needed to decide what to build, what to keep, and how to present it — the skill that becomes the primary bottleneck in product work once implementation (via AI) is nearly free.
- [[Teaching Hospital Model]] — [[Mark Pincus]]'s mentorship model for scaling a founder's product judgment through an organization: put as many people as possible in the room during product decisions (like medical students observing in a teaching hospital), then select someone from the ranks to become a dedicated "tech assistant" who shadows the founder for 6-12 months, absorbing their approach and passion (Pincus calls this "transferring your vampire blood") before being placed in a bigger independent role.
- [[Teams Optimize For Local Comfort]] — [[Matt MacInnis]]'s observation that teams will always drift toward optimizing for local comfort over company outcomes as an emergent property of human groups, not deliberate malice — the reason leaders must treat fighting organizational entropy as a daily task.
- [[Technical Debt]] — Tony Fadell's term for the long-term cost of AI-generated code and products that work in the short term but lack sound architecture.
- [[Terra Nova Narrative]] — [[Sam Lesson]]'s framing (referencing "Teranova," i.e., *Terra Nova*, new/undiscovered land) for a recurring generational pattern: each generation becomes desperate to believe in a new economic "frontier" — the American West, the internet, mobile, crypto, and now AI — and this desperation, more than the underlying economics, drives speculative investment into whichever frontier is currently fashionable.
- [[The Pickle (Product Quality List)]] — [[Rippling]]'s internal product-quality checklist, per [[Matt MacInnis]]: a deliberately quirky, memorable name chosen to drive cultural adoption of quality standards, iterated continuously as the team learns.
- [[Thin Template Pattern]] — [[Simon Willison]]'s practice of starting every new coding-agent project from a minimal, personally-styled project skeleton (e.g., a single passing test asserting 1+1=2, laid out exactly how he prefers) rather than a long written instructions file (like an extensive CLAUDE.md).
- [[Three Generations Rule]] — Tony Fadell's observed pattern that new hardware categories typically take three generations to succeed: "make the product, fix the product, then fix the business."
- [[Time Machine Moments]] — [[Zevi Arnovitz]]'s term for moments when he has multiple AI agents working in parallel across unrelated projects and, with nothing left to actively do, experiences a disorienting sense of having glimpsed the near future early.
- [[Tiny Core]] — [[Max Schoening]]'s theory that every truly great product succeeds because of one exceptionally good, narrow capability, not the accumulation of many good features.
- [[Token Budgeting]] — The practice, described by [[Adam Mosseri]] (Head of [[Instagram]]), of managing AI token/compute spend as a constrained organizational resource — analogous to GPUs, storage, labeling opex, or headcount payroll — rather than leaving it unmanaged or gamifying it via leaderboards.
- [[Torchbearers]] — One of the named governance frameworks [[Eric Ries]] formalizes in *[[Incorruptible (book)|Incorruptible]]* — individuals within an organization who personally carry and enforce the founding mission forward, particularly at succession points.
- [[Trust But Verify]] — Trust but verify is [[Fiona Fung]]'s stated philosophy for working with AI-generated code and output at [[Anthropic]]: the underlying models are described as very capable, but there remain areas — particularly ones requiring deep subject-matter/systems expertise — that still need active human verification rather than blind trust.
- [[Two-Week PM Rule]] — [[Amol]]'s heuristic for when a project needs dedicated PM ownership at [[Anthropic]]'s growth org: if a project is estimated at two engineering-weeks or less, the engineer is expected to act as their own mini-PM (including cross-functional coordination with legal, security, etc.), with the PM only advising; above two weeks, a PM remains squarely accountable, delegating execution to engineering but still owning the outcome.
- [[Ugly Baby]] — [[Keith Rabois]]'s contrarian investing heuristic, drawn from the idea (referenced via Ed Catmull's Pixar writing) that every great creative idea starts as an "ugly baby" no one wants to nurture: if fellow VCs would laugh at an investment, that's a positive signal, not a negative one, since it suggests real alpha (an idea the market hasn't correctly priced) rather than consensus competition.
- [[Under-Resourcing Principle]] — [[Boris Cherny]]'s deliberate practice of assigning fewer people to a project than it seemingly needs, forcing the team to lean on Claude to move fast — paired with giving engineers unrestricted token budgets rather than optimizing cost early.
- [[Undiscovered Talent]] — [[Keith Rabois]]'s recruiting philosophy, learned from [[Peter Thiel]] at [[PayPal]]: build a company around people the market hasn't correctly evaluated yet, rather than competing (and losing, on price) for already-proven "everybody wants them" hires.
- [[Utility Curves]] — [[Stewart Butterfield]]'s framework mapping how much value users get from a product against the effort or number of features required, arguing that adding features usually flattens rather than steepens the curve.
- [[Vibe Coding]] — Term for building software primarily by prompting/steering AI coding agents rather than writing code by hand.
- [[Vision Vs Strategy]] — A framework articulated by [[Adam Mosseri]] (Head of [[Instagram]]) distinguishing vision (an articulation of a desired future/product state) from strategy (an opinionated, necessarily contestable path toward that vision) — and arguing both are where human judgment will matter most as AI absorbs more of execution.
- [[Waterline Model]] — Framework by [[Molly Graham]] (referenced via a guest post on [[Lenny Rachitsky]]'s newsletter), cited in this episode as analogous to AI agent management: most team performance problems aren't really about a person's competence, but about structural gaps — unclear expectations, undefined success criteria, or overlapping ownership.
- [[We Don't Sell Saddles Here]] — [[Stewart Butterfield]]'s famous early internal memo arguing a product must be sold on the outcome/experience it enables — not on its literal feature — grounded in the book *[[Positioning (book)|Positioning]]*.
- [[Wizard Book Analogy]] — [[Sherwin Wu]]'s central metaphor for AI-native engineering, drawn from MIT's 1980s programming textbook *Structure and Interpretation of Computer Programs* (nicknamed "the wizard book"), which frames programmers as wizards, programming languages as incantations, and code as spells that go out and act in the world.
- [[Working Backwards]] — Amazon's product development methodology (associated with Jeff Bezos) of writing the product's press release before starting the project; echoed by Tony Fadell's own advice in *Build*.
- [[Zone Defense]] — "Zone defense" is [[Andrew Ambrosino]]'s sports analogy (developed with a colleague, Alexander) for how product people should organize themselves now that implementation is cheap and curation/[[Taste]] is the bottleneck: rather than clustering on the same idea, product people should spread out to cover distinct areas of the problem space.
- [[Zone Of Genius]] — [[Rachel Lockett]]'s burnout-prevention framework: people have more energy operating in their natural gifts, with an aspirational target of ~80% of work time spent there.
